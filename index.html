The course assumes a full year of high-school biology and a little chemistry, mostly as vocabulary and a mental map: what a macromolecule is, what a cell looks like from the inside, what DNA has to do with protein. That map is what's missing, not ability. Everyone who has it hears the lecture as a story; without it, the lecture is a list of words. The map is small, and short videos build it faster than reading does. Once you have it, the slides and homework get faster, which is where the time comes back.

What the syllabus actually says

Four things in the syllabus shape how to study. They are worth more than any resource below.

Slides beat the book

"Not all material presented will be taken from the textbook. Some exam content may relate directly to material discussed only in lecture." The textbook is recommended, not required. So the Canvas slides are the primary text; Freeman is the explainer you go to when a slide doesn't make sense.

Exam 1 is the low-stakes one

Four exams; your lowest counts 10% and the other three count 15% each. Exams are not cumulative. If Exam 1 goes badly, it becomes the 10% one and you still have 90% of the grade ahead of you. Worth real effort, but it cannot sink the course.

Quizzes come back as exam questions

Eight quizzes at the start of class, 2% each, lowest dropped. "Quiz questions may be used with modification as exam questions." Keep every quiz. Redo it from memory the week of the exam.

Lab is a quarter of the grade

Lab has its own Canvas site, its own TA, and its own director (Dr. Brenda Chow). It is 25%, more than any single exam. Treat lab handouts and lab practicals as a separate course with its own prep.

The syllabus bans generative AI for coursework "to build effective analytical and communication skills," so the two Current Events write-ups and anything else you submit need to be your own words. This page is a study schedule, not coursework, and it was built with AI help. That is worth knowing so you can decide for yourself how you want to use it.

The 35-minute block

Every sprint day, and most lecture days after that, is built from this block. Same order every time so it needs no decisions, unless you find a better order. Most days are one block plus the slides for that lecture, about an hour; the review day before the exam is three or four blocks, and each day below says how long it really is.

  1. 10 minWatch the day's video at 1.25× with the free recap handout open. Pause to fill it in. The handout is the point; the video is the delivery.
  2. 5 minSkim the slides for that lecture on Canvas, then the Key Concepts box and chapter summary in Freeman. Not the chapter itself yet.
  3. 15 minPractice the matching Khan Academy lesson until you get four questions right in a row. Wrong answers are the useful ones: read the explanation.
  4. 5 minWrite from memory, book closed: what this thing is, what it's made of, what it does. Then one question you still can't answer, for Thursday office hours or tutoring.

Two rules that matter more than the resources. Watching is not studying: the video earns nothing until the practice questions. And the video comes before the slides and the lecture, never as a replacement for them. Its job is to hang hooks that the lecture can then attach to.

If a day slips, do the minimum: the video and ten Khan questions. Twenty minutes, and it still counts. And this is a starting point, not a contract. After a week, keep what is working and drop what isn't.

Lab practicals

Lab is 25% of the grade, more than any single exam, and it runs as its own course: separate Canvas site, your TA, and Dr. Brenda Chow as lab director. The lecture syllabus says the lab assesses experimental design (replicates, control variables, error), building models through successive experiments, and quantitative and computer-based analysis. A lab practical tests whether you can do those things at a bench with a clock running, which is a different skill from the lecture exam and needs different prep.

First, get the format from the TA. In the next lab, ask: Is the practical stations with a timer? What counts as testable: structures on slides, procedures, calculations, reading data? Is there a list of specimens or structures we're expected to know? Can we bring anything? Everything below assumes a typical intro-bio practical; the TA's answer overrides it.

What a practical usually looks like

A ring of numbered stations, two or three minutes each, one to three questions per station, and you rotate on a bell. A station is a microscope with a slide and a pointer ("what is the structure at the pointer, and what does it do?"), a piece of equipment ("what is this, and what would you use it for?"), a data table or graph ("which variable is independent; what does this result mean?"), a calculation (magnification, a dilution, a unit conversion), or a procedure question ("why did we add the buffer before the enzyme?"). There is no going back to a station, so you cannot leave one blank and return.

How to prepare, lab by lab

  • Same day as each lab, 15 minutes: one page in a notebook with five things: the question the lab was asking, the why of each step (not the recipe), the equipment used and what it's for, a labelled sketch of what you saw, and one sentence of conclusion. Practicals test the why; the recipe is on the handout.
  • Draw what you saw. Every slide, every specimen: a sketch with labels and the magnification written next to it. Before the practical, redraw each from memory and check. Diagram labelling is on the lecture exams too, so this pays twice.
  • The post-lab questions are the question bank. Redo every one closed-book the week of the practical. Practical questions are usually those questions with the numbers or the specimen changed.
  • Build your own stations. Five days out, make ten stations from the lab handouts: a photo, a diagram, a data table, a calculation, one per index card or phone photo. Two minutes each, write the answer, move on, no going back. Trade stations with a lab partner so you each get ten you didn't write.
  • Start five days out. A practical covers several labs at once. It cannot be done the night before, and the lab syllabus, not this one, says whether anything is dropped; assume nothing is.

The skills that show up on every intro-bio practical

  • Microscope. Parts and what each does; total magnification is ocular times objective; how to focus from low power up; making a wet mount; estimating the size of a cell from the field of view. Amoeba Sisters · Microscopes and How to Use a Light Microscope 9:16, and Crash Course #22 · Microscopes for the why.
  • Experimental design. Hypothesis versus prediction; independent, dependent and controlled variables; positive and negative controls; why replicates; what "error" means. Amoeba Sisters · Nature of Science and Crash Course #2 · The Scientific Method.
  • Writing a conclusion. Claim, evidence, reasoning, in that order, in three sentences. Amoeba Sisters · CER in Biology 7:39. This is also how the lab report questions want to be answered.
  • Numbers. Metric prefixes and conversions (milli, micro, nano); a dilution; reading and drawing a graph with labelled axes, units and error bars; mean and a rough sense of spread. Khan Academy has these inside its lessons; Freeman's BioSkills section covers the metric system, graphs and basic statistics in a few pages.
  • Safety and equipment. Where things are and why the rule exists. Amoeba Sisters · General Lab Safety 5:54.

Lab questions go to your TA or Dr. Chow, not Dr. Krogan, and the lab has its own policies on missed sessions. Read the lab syllabus on the lab Canvas site the same way you read this one.

Self-testing toolkit

Reading feels like learning and isn't. Answering questions with the book closed is what moves things into memory, and it is also the only way to find out what you don't know before the exam does. Everything here is free.

  • The slides and the quizzes. Not a website, but the top of this list. Question-per-slide through every deck, and every quiz redone from memory, is the closest thing to the real exam that exists.
  • Khan Academy AP/College Biology. Every lesson in Units 1–6 has a practice set; every unit has a unit test. Make a free account so it tracks what you've mastered. The "AP" label is only the level: it matches BIO 110 well.
  • OpenStax Biology 2e. A free, peer-reviewed intro textbook that covers the same ground as Freeman. Each chapter ends with review questions and critical-thinking questions, but OpenStax publishes no answer key, so treat them as extra questions to check against Freeman or the slides, not as a self-grading quiz. Its real value is as a second telling when Freeman's explanation doesn't land.
  • Amoeba Sisters recap handouts. Free one-page fill-in sheets for each video. Fill it in during the video, then a week later redo it blank.
  • Freeman's own questions. The "Check Your Understanding" boxes inside each chapter and the "Test Your Knowledge" questions at the end. The Pearson eText also has built-in flashcards and full-text search, which beats scrolling. Mastering Biology is a separate Pearson product; the syllabus doesn't mention it, so assume the course doesn't use it.
  • Flashcards, your own. Anki (free on computer and Android) or Quizlet. Five cards per lecture, a question on the front, one fact on the back. Cards you make yourself beat any downloaded deck, because making the card is half the studying.
  • HHMI BioInteractive. Free animations and click-and-learn interactives. Go here when a static diagram won't resolve into a picture, especially for membranes, respiration and DNA.
  • Learn.Genetics. University of Utah's interactives on cells and genetics. Short and visual.

Phase 3 · After Exam 1

The first exam is the data, and the syllabus makes it the cheapest data you will get all semester: if it is your lowest, it counts 10%. If the score is at or above the class median, keep only the weekly rhythm. If it is below, keep the rhythm, add a second self-test session mid-week, and make the PASS appointment a fixed part of the week. Either way, go back through the exam question by question and sort every miss into "didn't know it" or "knew it, misread the question". The two need different fixes, and most people have more of the second than they expect.
