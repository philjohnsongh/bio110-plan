Chapter 1Biology: The Study of Life

The forest

Chapter 1 is not really about facts. It is about the three big ideas the whole course rests on, and about how biologists decide what is true. The ideas: everything alive is made of cells, and every cell came from another cell. Living things change over generations because the individuals that happen to survive and reproduce pass on whatever helped them; that is evolution by natural selection. And all living things are related, on one enormous family tree with three main branches. The method: a guess you can test (a hypothesis), a specific thing that should happen if the guess is right (a prediction), and an experiment with a comparison group (a control) so you can tell whether the thing you changed made the difference.

If you take one sentence from the chapter: life is cells, cells come from cells, and the differences between living things come from descent with modification.

Celland cell theory

In plain words

A cell is the smallest thing that is alive on its own: a tiny bag of watery stuff with a skin around it, that can take in food, make copies of itself, and keep itself going. Everything alive is either one cell or a pile of them. Cell theory is the two-part rule: all living things are made of cells, and every cell comes from a cell that already existed. Cells don't pop up from nothing.

Picture
The technical version

The cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of life: a membrane-bound compartment containing cytoplasm and genetic material. Cell theory states that all organisms are composed of one or more cells and that all cells arise from pre-existing cells by division. The course will spend Chapters 6 and 7 on the membrane and the inside of the cell.

Evolutionnatural selection · adaptation · fitness

In plain words

Evolution just means that a population of living things changes over generations. Natural selection is the main reason it happens: individuals vary a little, some of that variation is inherited, and the ones whose variation happens to help them survive and have offspring leave more copies of themselves. Over many generations the helpful version becomes common. Nobody is trying; it's arithmetic. An adaptation is a trait that got common that way because it helps. Fitness, in biology, isn't about being strong. It means how many offspring an individual leaves compared to others.

Picture
The technical version

Evolution is change in the heritable characteristics of a population across generations. Natural selection occurs when heritable variation in a trait causes differential reproductive success; the trait that increases fitness (reproductive success relative to others) increases in frequency. A trait that spreads because it increases fitness is an adaptation. Freeman's phrase: descent with modification.

Tree of lifephylogeny · species · the three domains

In plain words

Every living thing is related to every other, the way you are related to your cousins: through shared ancestors further back. Draw all those relationships and you get one giant family tree, the tree of life. A phylogeny is any piece of that tree. A species is, roughly, one twig: a group that breeds among itself. The three biggest branches are called domains: Bacteria, Archaea (which look like bacteria but are chemically a different lineage), and Eukarya (everything with a nucleus in its cells, including plants, fungi, animals, and you).

Picture
The technical version

The tree of life is the phylogeny of all organisms, inferred largely from comparing gene sequences (originally ribosomal RNA, by Woese). Its three domains are Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. Bacteria and Archaea are prokaryotic (no nucleus); Eukarya are eukaryotic. A species is commonly defined as a group of interbreeding populations reproductively isolated from others, though the course will show that this definition has edges.

Prokaryote and eukaryote

In plain words

Two ways to build a cell. A prokaryote keeps its DNA loose in the main compartment, like a studio apartment: one room, everything in it. A eukaryote keeps its DNA inside a walled-off inner room, the nucleus, and has other little rooms with jobs, like a house. Bacteria and Archaea are prokaryotes. Everything else, including every cell in your body, is a eukaryote. The prefix tells you: pro- "before", eu- "true", karyon "kernel", so "before a kernel" versus "true kernel".

Picture
The technical version

Prokaryotic cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus; their chromosome sits in a region of cytoplasm called the nucleoid, and they generally lack membrane-bound organelles. Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus enclosed by a double membrane and a set of membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi, and so on). Eukaryotic cells are typically much larger. Chapter 7 details both.

Hypothesis, prediction, experiment, control

In plain words

A hypothesis is a proposed explanation you can test: "the plant died because it got too little light." A prediction is what you should see if the hypothesis is right: "plants in the shade will grow less than plants in the sun." The experiment is the setup that checks the prediction. The control is the comparison group where you change nothing, so that if the shaded plants grow less you can say it was the shade and not the pot or the water. Without a control you can't tell what caused what.

Picture
The technical version

A hypothesis is a testable explanation for an observation; a prediction is a measurable outcome that must hold if the hypothesis is correct. A controlled experiment varies one factor (the independent variable) between a treatment group and a control group, holds other conditions constant, and measures the response (the dependent variable). Replicates and controls let you distinguish an effect from chance. Freeman uses the null hypothesis, the "no effect" alternative, as the comparison.

Theoryand why it doesn't mean "guess"

In plain words

In everyday English a theory is a hunch. In science it is the opposite: an explanation that has survived so many tests that it organizes a whole field. Cell theory and the theory of evolution are theories in that sense, the same sense as the theory of gravity. A hypothesis is one testable guess; a theory is a big, well-tested explanation that many hypotheses hang from. The word trap is real and it shows up on exams.

Picture
Hold this image: a hypothesis is a single brick you are testing to see if it holds weight. A theory is a building that has stood for a century, made of thousands of bricks that all held. Nobody calls the building "just a brick."
The technical version

A scientific theory is a broad explanatory framework supported by a large body of evidence and capable of generating testable hypotheses. It is not provisional in the everyday sense; it can be refined but is not overturned by a single result. Contrast: hypothesis (specific, testable, provisional) and law (a description of a regularity, not an explanation).

Chapter 2Water and Carbon: The Chemical Basis of Life

The forest

This chapter is the chemistry the course assumes you had. The whole thing comes down to one idea: atoms stick to each other in a few different ways, and the way they stick decides everything else. Atoms share electrons (a covalent bond), or one steals from another (an ionic bond). When two atoms share unevenly, the molecule gets a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end (it is polar), and polar molecules are attracted to each other (that attraction is a hydrogen bond). Water is polar, which is why it clings to itself, dissolves things, and resists changing temperature. Every property of water in the chapter is that one fact wearing a different hat.

Carbon gets the other half of the chapter because a carbon atom can make four bonds, so it can build chains and rings, so it can build the big molecules of Chapters 3 through 6. The functional groups are the little attachments on those carbon skeletons that give each molecule its personality.

One sentence: uneven sharing of electrons makes water sticky; carbon's four bonds make big molecules possible.

Atomproton · neutron · electron · element

In plain words

An atom is the smallest piece of a substance that is still that substance. It has a heavy center (protons, which are positive, and neutrons, which are neutral) and a cloud of much lighter electrons (negative) around it. The number of protons decides which element it is: 6 protons is always carbon, 8 is always oxygen. Electrons are the part that does chemistry, because they are on the outside and can be shared or handed over.

Picture
The technical version

An atom consists of a nucleus of protons (charge +1) and neutrons (charge 0), surrounded by electrons (charge −1) occupying orbitals grouped into shells. Atomic number = number of protons and defines the element; mass number = protons + neutrons. Atoms of the same element with different neutron numbers are isotopes. Chemical behaviour is determined by the electrons in the outermost (valence) shell.

Valenceelectron shells · why atoms bond at all

In plain words

Electrons sit in layers (shells) around the nucleus, and each layer has a fixed number of seats. Atoms are "happiest" when the outer layer is full. Most of them aren't, so they bond with other atoms to share or swap electrons until the outer layer fills up. Valence is the number of bonds an atom makes to get there: hydrogen 1, oxygen 2, nitrogen 3, carbon 4. That single number explains the shape of every molecule in the course.

Picture
The technical version

Electrons occupy shells of increasing energy; the first holds 2, the second 8. Atoms are most stable with a filled valence (outermost) shell, and the number of unpaired valence electrons sets an atom's valence, the number of covalent bonds it typically forms: H 1, O 2, N 3, C 4. Unpaired valence electrons are what bonding pairs up.

Covalent bondmolecule · nonpolar vs polar

In plain words

Two atoms both need an electron, so they share a pair between them. That shared pair is a covalent bond, and it is strong: it holds molecules together. A molecule is just atoms held together by covalent bonds. If the two atoms pull on the shared pair equally, the bond is nonpolar (like two people carrying a box evenly). If one atom pulls harder, the electrons spend more time near it, so that end goes slightly negative and the other end slightly positive: the bond is polar. Oxygen and nitrogen pull hard; carbon and hydrogen pull about the same as each other.

Picture
The technical version

A covalent bond is a shared pair of valence electrons between two atoms; a molecule is two or more atoms joined covalently. Electronegativity is an atom's tendency to attract shared electrons (O and N high; C and H similar and moderate). Equal sharing gives a nonpolar covalent bond (C–H, C–C); unequal sharing gives a polar covalent bond (O–H, N–H) with partial charges δ− on the more electronegative atom and δ+ on the other. Double and triple bonds share two or three pairs.

Ionic bondion · cation · anion

In plain words

Sometimes one atom doesn't share; it takes an electron outright. The taker ends up with an extra negative charge, the giver with a missing one, so now one is negative and one is positive, and opposite charges attract. That attraction is an ionic bond. An atom with a charge is an ion: a positive one is a cation, a negative one an anion. Table salt is sodium (gave one away, now +) stuck to chloride (took it, now −). In water, ionic bonds come apart easily, which is why salt dissolves.

Picture
The technical version

An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed when one atom transfers one or more electrons to another. Cations carry net positive charge (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺); anions net negative (Cl⁻). In aqueous solution the ions are surrounded by water molecules (hydration shells) and dissociate. Many biologically important ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, H⁺) appear throughout the course.

Hydrogen bond

In plain words

This is the one the chapter turns on, and the name is misleading: it is not a bond inside a molecule. It is a weak attraction between molecules. When a hydrogen is attached to an oxygen or nitrogen, it is left slightly positive (the O or N hogs the electrons). That slightly positive hydrogen is attracted to a slightly negative oxygen or nitrogen on a neighbouring molecule. One hydrogen bond is weak, like a Velcro hook. Millions of them together are strong, like a whole strip of Velcro. Water sticks to itself with them; DNA's two strands are held together with them; proteins hold their shape with them.

Picture
The technical version

A hydrogen bond is a weak electrostatic attraction between a partially positive hydrogen atom that is covalently bonded to an electronegative atom (O or N) and a partially negative O or N on another molecule or another part of the same molecule. Individually weak (about 1/20 of a covalent bond) and constantly breaking and re-forming, hydrogen bonds are collectively responsible for water's cohesion, the pairing of DNA strands, and protein secondary structure.

Water's propertiescohesion · adhesion · surface tension · specific heat

In plain words

Every odd thing water does is hydrogen bonding wearing a different hat. Water molecules cling to each other (cohesion), which is why a drop holds together and why the surface acts like a stretched skin (surface tension). They also cling to other polar surfaces (adhesion), which is why water creeps up a paper towel. It takes a lot of heat to warm water up, because the heat first has to shake the hydrogen bonds loose (high specific heat), which is why oceans and your body resist temperature swings. And ice floats because the hydrogen bonds lock the molecules into an open lattice that is less dense than the liquid.

Picture
The technical version

Cohesion: attraction between like molecules, here water to water via hydrogen bonding, producing surface tension. Adhesion: attraction between unlike molecules, e.g. water to polar surfaces. Specific heat: energy required to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 °C; water's is high because energy goes into breaking hydrogen bonds before raising kinetic energy. Heat of vaporization is high for the same reason (evaporative cooling). Ice is less dense than liquid water because hydrogen bonds hold molecules in an open crystal lattice. Water is also an excellent solvent for polar and charged solutes.

Hydrophilic and hydrophobicsolvent · solute · solution

In plain words

A solvent is the liquid doing the dissolving; a solute is the thing dissolved in it; together they are a solution. In biology the solvent is almost always water. Water dissolves things that are polar or charged, because its own partial charges can surround them and pull them apart; such things are hydrophilic, "water-loving": salt, sugar, most of what's in a cell. Things that are nonpolar, like oil and fat, have no charges for water to grab, so water molecules would rather hold hands with each other and the oil gets pushed aside; those are hydrophobic, "water-fearing". Hydrophobic doesn't mean the oil is afraid; it means water excludes it. This one idea builds cell membranes in Chapter 6 and folds proteins in Chapter 3.

Picture
The technical version

Hydrophilic substances are polar or ionic and interact favourably with water, dissolving as water molecules form hydration shells around them. Hydrophobic substances are nonpolar and do not interact with water; they aggregate because that arrangement lets water maximise its own hydrogen bonding (the hydrophobic effect, an entropy-driven process). A solution is a homogeneous mixture of solute in solvent; concentration is often given in molarity (moles of solute per litre).

Acid, base, and pHbuffer

In plain words

Water molecules occasionally split into a hydrogen ion (H⁺, a bare proton) and a hydroxide ion (OH⁻). An acid is anything that adds H⁺ to a solution; a base is anything that removes it. pH is a scale for how much H⁺ there is: 7 is neutral (pure water), lower is more acidic, higher is more basic. It runs backwards and in powers of ten: pH 3 has ten times more H⁺ than pH 4, and a thousand times more than pH 6. Your blood is held at about 7.4 by buffers, molecules that soak up or release H⁺ to stop the number drifting, because proteins fall apart if it does.

Picture
The technical version

Water dissociates slightly: H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻ (the proton is actually carried as hydronium, H₃O⁺). An acid donates protons, raising [H⁺]; a base accepts protons, lowering it. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; at 25 °C pure water has [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷ M, so pH 7. A buffer is a weak acid/conjugate base pair that minimises pH change by absorbing or releasing H⁺; the carbonic acid/bicarbonate system buffers blood near 7.4.

Carbon and organic moleculescarbon skeleton · hydrocarbon

In plain words

"Organic" in chemistry has nothing to do with farming. It means "contains carbon bonded to hydrogen." Carbon is the building material of life because it makes four bonds, so carbon atoms can link into long chains, branches and rings, and hang other atoms off the sides. That chain or ring is the carbon skeleton; the molecule is built on it. A hydrocarbon is a skeleton with only hydrogens attached, like the stuff in gasoline; it is nonpolar, so it is hydrophobic. Add oxygen or nitrogen somewhere and the molecule gets a polar spot and a personality.

Picture
The technical version

Organic molecules contain carbon covalently bonded to hydrogen. Carbon's valence of four and its ability to form single, double and triple bonds with itself and with H, O, N, S and P allow diverse skeletons: linear, branched, and ring structures. Hydrocarbons are nonpolar. The chapter also introduces Freeman's theme of chemical evolution: the hypothesis that simple carbon compounds formed and grew more complex under early-Earth conditions before life existed.

Functional groups

In plain words

A functional group is a small cluster of atoms that hangs off a carbon skeleton and gives the molecule a specific behaviour. Think of the skeleton as a phone and the functional groups as the apps: the phone does nothing by itself, the apps make it useful. There are six the course cares about. A hydroxyl (–OH) makes a spot polar and water-friendly. A carboxyl (–COOH) acts as an acid. An amino (–NH₂) acts as a base. A phosphate (–OPO₃²⁻) carries negative charge and energy. A carbonyl (C=O) is a reactive spot found in sugars. A sulfhydryl (–SH) can lock onto another sulfhydryl to pin a protein's shape.

Picture
The technical version

Functional groups are specific groups of atoms with characteristic chemical properties regardless of the skeleton they attach to. Hydroxyl (–OH): polar, hydrogen-bonds. Carbonyl (C=O): aldehyde if terminal, ketone if internal; reactive. Carboxyl (–COOH): weak acid, ionises to –COO⁻. Amino (–NH₂): weak base, protonates to –NH₃⁺. Phosphate (–OPO₃²⁻): negatively charged; links nucleotides and stores energy in ATP. Sulfhydryl (–SH): forms disulfide bridges (S–S) between cysteines in proteins. Methyl (–CH₃) is nonpolar and appears in later chapters as a regulatory tag.

Chemical reaction and energyreactants · products · potential energy · spontaneous

In plain words

A chemical reaction is atoms rearranging: bonds break, different bonds form. What you start with are the reactants, what you end with are the products. Energy is stored in bonds the way energy is stored in a stretched spring (that is potential energy), and different bonds store different amounts, so a reaction either releases energy or needs energy put in. A reaction that runs on its own, releasing energy and usually making things more disordered, is called spontaneous. "Spontaneous" says nothing about speed: rusting is spontaneous and takes years. The chapter's deeper point is that the universe drifts toward lower energy and more disorder, and life is the trick of using energy flow to build order anyway.

Picture
The technical version

In a chemical reaction, reactants are converted to products by breaking and forming covalent bonds. Potential energy is stored in chemical bonds; kinetic energy is energy of motion (including thermal energy). Freeman introduces Gibbs free energy: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, where ΔH is change in enthalpy (heat content) and ΔS change in entropy (disorder). Reactions with ΔG < 0 are exergonic and spontaneous; ΔG > 0 endergonic, requiring energy input. The second law of thermodynamics states that total entropy increases. Spontaneity is independent of rate, which is set by activation energy (Chapter 3, enzymes).

Chapter 3Protein Structure and Function

The forest

Proteins are the machines of the cell: the things that do stuff. They carry oxygen, cut and paste other molecules, form muscle, recognise invaders, run every chemical reaction in you. The whole chapter is one idea told at four levels: a protein is a chain of small units (amino acids), and the order of the units decides how the chain folds, and the fold decides what the protein can do. Shape is function. Change the shape, whether by heat, acid, or one wrong unit, and the machine stops working.

The second half of the chapter is about the most important job proteins do: acting as enzymes, which make specific reactions happen millions of times faster by holding the reacting molecules in exactly the right position. An enzyme doesn't add energy; it lowers the hill.

One sentence: twenty kinds of bead, strung in an order, fold into a shape, and the shape is the job.

Monomer and polymercondensation (dehydration) reaction · hydrolysis

In plain words

A monomer is one bead; a polymer is a string of beads. Proteins, DNA, and starch are all polymers, each made from its own kind of bead. The cell links two beads by pulling an H off one and an OH off the other and joining what's left; the H and OH leave together as a water molecule. Because a water is removed, the joining reaction is called a condensation or dehydration reaction. Taking the string apart is the reverse: put a water back in and the link breaks. That is hydrolysis ("water-splitting"), which is what digestion is.

Picture
The technical version

Monomers are small molecular subunits; polymers are chains of monomers joined by covalent bonds. Polymerisation proceeds by condensation (dehydration) reactions, in which a hydroxyl from one monomer and a hydrogen from another are removed as water and a covalent bond forms. Hydrolysis reverses this by adding water. Condensation is endergonic and in cells is coupled to energy sources (ATP); hydrolysis is exergonic. The specific bonds are peptide bonds (proteins), phosphodiester bonds (nucleic acids), and glycosidic linkages (carbohydrates).

Amino acidR-group (side chain)

In plain words

The bead that proteins are made of. Every amino acid has the same core: a central carbon with an amino group on one side, a carboxyl group on the other, a hydrogen, and one variable part called the R-group or side chain. The twenty amino acids differ only in that side chain. Some side chains are oily (hydrophobic), some are polar, some carry a charge. That variety is the point: it's what lets a chain fold into a specific shape, because the oily ones hide inside and the polar ones face the water.

Picture
The technical version

An amino acid has a central α-carbon bonded to an amino group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen, and a side chain (R-group). At cellular pH the amino group is protonated (–NH₃⁺) and the carboxyl deprotonated (–COO⁻). The 20 standard amino acids are grouped by R-group chemistry: nonpolar (hydrophobic), polar uncharged, and charged (acidic or basic). Side-chain properties drive folding and determine function.

Peptide bondpolypeptide · protein

In plain words

The link between two amino acids. The carboxyl end of one joins the amino end of the next, a water leaves, and the bond that forms is a peptide bond. String enough together and you have a polypeptide: a chain with a definite direction, an amino end and a carboxyl end. A protein is one or more polypeptides folded into a working shape. Chains are anywhere from a few dozen to thousands of amino acids long.

Picture
The technical version

A peptide bond is the covalent C–N bond formed by condensation between the carboxyl carbon of one amino acid and the amino nitrogen of the next. It has partial double-bond character, so it is planar and rigid, which constrains folding. A polypeptide has an N-terminus and a C-terminus; the repeating N–Cα–C atoms form the backbone, and R-groups project from it. Proteins are functional polypeptides; oligopeptides are short chains.

The four levels of protein structureprimary · secondary (α-helix, β-sheet) · tertiary · quaternary

In plain words

Four ways to describe the same protein, zooming out. Primary: the order of the beads, like the letters in a word. Secondary: short stretches of the chain that coil into a spiral (an alpha helix) or fold back and forth into a flat pleat (a beta sheet), held by hydrogen bonds along the backbone. Tertiary: the whole chain folded up into its 3-D shape, held mostly by the side chains, with oily ones tucked inside away from water. Quaternary: some proteins are several folded chains clicked together; that arrangement is the fourth level. Not every protein has a quaternary level; every protein has the first three.

Picture
The technical version

Primary structure: the amino acid sequence, joined by peptide bonds. Secondary structure: regular local patterns, the α-helix and β-pleated sheet, stabilised by hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H groups. Tertiary structure: the overall three-dimensional fold of one polypeptide, stabilised by side-chain interactions: hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, van der Waals interactions, and covalent disulfide bridges. Quaternary structure: the association of two or more polypeptide subunits (e.g. haemoglobin, four subunits). Sequence determines structure; structure determines function.

Folding, shape, and denaturation

In plain words

A chain of amino acids does not stay a floppy string. In water it folds itself, in a fraction of a second, into one particular shape, because its oily side chains huddle away from water and its polar ones reach toward it. That shape is what does the job: a pocket that fits one molecule, a groove that grips DNA, a hinge that opens. Heat, acid, or the wrong chemical can shake the folds apart; that is denaturation, and a denatured protein has stopped working even though every bead is still there. A cooked egg white is denatured protein: same molecules, wrong shape, no going back.

Picture
The technical version

Folding is spontaneous and driven largely by the hydrophobic effect, with the final conformation stabilised by the interactions listed under tertiary structure; chaperone proteins assist folding in cells. Denaturation is loss of native conformation, and therefore function, without breaking peptide bonds, caused by heat, pH change, or chemicals that disrupt non-covalent interactions. It can be reversible for small proteins (Anfinsen's ribonuclease experiment showed sequence alone specifies the fold) but is often irreversible in practice. Misfolded proteins that propagate their shape are prions.

Enzymecatalyst · substrate · active site · activation energy

In plain words

An enzyme is a protein whose job is to make one specific reaction happen fast. Most reactions in a cell would take years on their own, not because they're uphill but because the molecules need a jolt to get started, the way a match needs striking. That starting jolt is the activation energy. An enzyme has a pocket (the active site) shaped to hold the reacting molecule (the substrate) in exactly the position where the reaction is easiest, so the jolt needed is much smaller and the reaction runs millions of times faster. The enzyme itself is unchanged afterwards and goes again; that is what "catalyst" means. Each enzyme fits one substrate, which is why the cell needs thousands of them.

Picture
The technical version

A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction without being consumed and without changing ΔG. Enzymes are protein catalysts (some RNAs also catalyse; see ribozyme). The substrate binds the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex; binding often involves induced fit, a conformational change that tightens the fit. The enzyme lowers activation energy (E_a) by stabilising the transition state, orienting substrates, and providing a favourable microenvironment. Rate depends on substrate concentration, temperature and pH; many enzymes need cofactors (metal ions) or coenzymes (organic, often vitamin-derived). Regulation by inhibitors and feedback comes later in the course.

Chapter 4Nucleic Acids and an RNA World

The forest

DNA is a set of instructions written in a four-letter alphabet, and the chapter is about the alphabet's physical form. Each letter is a nucleotide: a sugar, a phosphate, and one of four bases. Nucleotides link into a strand with a backbone of sugar-phosphate-sugar-phosphate and the bases sticking out like the teeth of a zipper. Two strands zip together because each base has one partner it hydrogen-bonds with: A with T, G with C. That pairing rule is the whole reason DNA can be copied: pull the strands apart and each one dictates its partner.

RNA is DNA's single-stranded cousin, with one different sugar and one different letter (U instead of T). Because it is single-stranded it can fold up on itself into shapes, and shapes can do chemistry, so RNA can act as an enzyme. The "RNA world" is the idea that before there were proteins or DNA, RNA did both jobs, storing information and catalysing reactions. That is Freeman's hypothesis for how life got started, and it explains why this chapter comes before the cell.

One sentence: four letters on a sugar-phosphate string, paired A–T and G–C, so the message copies itself.

Nucleotidephosphate · sugar · nitrogenous base

In plain words

The bead that DNA and RNA are made of, and it has three parts stuck together: a phosphate group (the charged, energy-carrying attachment from Chapter 2), a five-carbon sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), and a base, which is the "letter" part, a flat ring containing nitrogen. Sugar and phosphate are the same in every bead of a strand; only the base changes. So a strand is a monotonous backbone with a message written on the side.

Picture
The technical version

A nucleotide consists of a phosphate group, a pentose sugar (ribose in RNA; 2′-deoxyribose in DNA, lacking the 2′ –OH), and a nitrogenous base attached at the sugar's 1′ carbon. The phosphate is attached at 5′; the 3′ carbon carries the hydroxyl that forms the next linkage. Sugar carbons are numbered with primes to distinguish them from base atoms. A nucleoside is sugar plus base without phosphate. Nucleoside triphosphates (ATP, GTP) are the activated monomers used for polymerisation and, in ATP's case, the cell's energy currency.

The basesA, T, G, C, U · purine vs pyrimidine · complementary pairing

In plain words

Four letters in DNA: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), cytosine (C). RNA swaps T for uracil (U). They come in two sizes: A and G are big (two rings, called purines); T, C and U are small (one ring, pyrimidines). The pairing rule follows from the sizes and from where their hydrogen-bond spots are: a big one always pairs with a small one, and specifically A with T (two hydrogen bonds) and G with C (three). "Complementary" means "the matching partner": if one strand reads ATGC, its partner reads TACG. That is the rule that makes copying possible.

Picture
The technical version

Purines (adenine, guanine) are double-ring bases; pyrimidines (cytosine, thymine, uracil) are single-ring. Complementary base pairing via hydrogen bonds: A pairs with T (or U in RNA) by two hydrogen bonds; G pairs with C by three. Purine–pyrimidine pairing keeps the double helix at constant diameter (Chargaff's rules: [A]=[T], [G]=[C]). GC-rich regions are more thermally stable. Uracil replaces thymine in RNA.

Backbone and directionphosphodiester bond · sugar-phosphate backbone · 5′ and 3′ ends · antiparallel

In plain words

Beads link handle to handle: the phosphate of one nucleotide bonds to the sugar of the next, so the strand's spine is sugar-phosphate-sugar-phosphate all the way along, with bases sticking out sideways. That link is a phosphodiester bond. Because each sugar has its phosphate on one corner (called 5′, "five-prime") and its free hook on another (3′), a strand has a direction: it starts at a 5′ end and finishes at a 3′ end, like a sentence read left to right. In double-stranded DNA the two strands run in opposite directions, one 5′→3′ and the other 3′→5′; that is what "antiparallel" means. Chapters 15 to 17 depend on this, because the copying machinery can only build in one direction.

The technical version

A phosphodiester bond links the 3′ hydroxyl of one nucleotide's sugar to the 5′ phosphate of the next, forming a directional sugar-phosphate backbone with a free 5′ phosphate at one end and a free 3′ hydroxyl at the other. Sequences are written 5′→3′. Polymerases add nucleotides only to the 3′ end, so strands grow 5′→3′. In DNA the two strands are antiparallel and complementary, wound into a right-handed double helix (Watson and Crick, 1953) with the bases stacked inside and the backbones outside; the helix has a major and a minor groove where proteins read the sequence.

DNA vs RNAdouble helix · template · gene

In plain words

DNA is two strands zipped into a twisted ladder (the double helix): sturdy, stable, good for long-term storage of the instructions. RNA is one strand, with a slightly different sugar (ribose, with one extra oxygen) and U in place of T. Being single, RNA can fold back on itself and pair with its own bases, making shapes, which lets it do jobs rather than just store text. The relationship is: DNA is the master copy kept in the archive; RNA is the working copy that gets sent out. A gene is a stretch of DNA that holds the instructions for one product, usually a protein. Because each strand pairs only with its complement, either strand can serve as a template for building the other, which is how DNA copies itself and how RNA gets made from it.

Picture
The technical version

DNA: 2′-deoxyribose, bases A T G C, double-stranded antiparallel helix; chemically stable (the missing 2′ –OH makes it resistant to hydrolysis), which suits information storage. RNA: ribose, bases A U G C, usually single-stranded but with extensive intramolecular base pairing producing secondary structure (hairpins, stem-loops) and tertiary structure; more reactive, and functional as messenger, structural, and catalytic molecules. A template strand directs synthesis of a complementary strand by base pairing; this underlies replication (Chapter 15) and transcription (Chapter 17). A gene is a DNA sequence encoding a functional product, usually a polypeptide or an RNA.

RNA worldribozyme · chemical evolution

In plain words

A chicken-and-egg problem: proteins are needed to copy DNA, but DNA is needed to make proteins. So which came first? The RNA-world hypothesis says neither. RNA can store information (it has bases that pair) and RNA can fold into shapes that do chemistry (an RNA enzyme is a ribozyme), so early life could have run on RNA alone, with DNA and proteins arriving later as specialists. The evidence includes ribozymes still working in your cells today: the machine that builds every protein in you, the ribosome, is at its core an RNA catalyst. Chemical evolution is Freeman's broader term for the whole idea that simple molecules on the early Earth grew more complex, step by step, until something could copy itself.

Picture
Hold this image: a recipe card that can also cook. DNA is only a recipe; a protein is only a cook; RNA, folded up, can be both, badly. "Badly" is enough to get started, and then the specialists take over.
The technical version

The RNA-world hypothesis proposes that self-replicating RNA molecules preceded DNA and proteins as the first information-carrying and catalytic molecules. Support: RNA's capacity for complementary base pairing (information) and for folding into catalytic structures (ribozymes, discovered by Cech and Altman); in vitro selection of RNAs that catalyse their own partial replication; and the peptidyl transferase activity of the ribosome residing in rRNA. Chemical evolution refers to the abiotic formation of increasingly complex carbon compounds, including nucleotides, under early-Earth conditions, followed by polymerisation.
