Chapter 1Biology: The Study of Life

The forest

Chapter 1 is not really about facts. It is about the three big ideas the whole course rests on, and about how biologists decide what is true. The ideas: everything alive is made of cells, and every cell came from another cell. Living things change over generations because the individuals that happen to survive and reproduce pass on whatever helped them; that is evolution by natural selection. And all living things are related, on one enormous family tree with three main branches. The method: a guess you can test (a hypothesis), a specific thing that should happen if the guess is right (a prediction), and an experiment with a comparison group (a control) so you can tell whether the thing you changed made the difference.

If you take one sentence from the chapter: life is cells, cells come from cells, and the differences between living things come from descent with modification.

Nature of Science · Biological Levels

Celland cell theory

In plain words

A cell is the smallest thing that is alive on its own: a tiny bag of watery stuff with a skin around it, that can take in food, make copies of itself, and keep itself going. Everything alive is either one cell or a pile of them. Cell theory is the two-part rule: all living things are made of cells, and every cell comes from a cell that already existed. Cells don't pop up from nothing.

Picture
The technical version

The cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of life: a membrane-bound compartment containing cytoplasm and genetic material. Cell theory states that all organisms are composed of one or more cells and that all cells arise from pre-existing cells by division. The course will spend Chapters 6 and 7 on the membrane and the inside of the cell.

Evolutionnatural selection · adaptation · fitness

In plain words

Evolution just means that a population of living things changes over generations. Natural selection is the main reason it happens: individuals vary a little, some of that variation is inherited, and the ones whose variation happens to help them survive and have offspring leave more copies of themselves. Over many generations the helpful version becomes common. Nobody is trying; it's arithmetic. An adaptation is a trait that got common that way because it helps. Fitness, in biology, isn't about being strong. It means how many offspring an individual leaves compared to others.

Picture
The technical version

Evolution is change in the heritable characteristics of a population across generations. Natural selection occurs when heritable variation in a trait causes differential reproductive success; the trait that increases fitness (reproductive success relative to others) increases in frequency. A trait that spreads because it increases fitness is an adaptation. Freeman's phrase: descent with modification.

Tree of lifephylogeny · species · the three domains

In plain words

Every living thing is related to every other, the way you are related to your cousins: through shared ancestors further back. Draw all those relationships and you get one giant family tree, the tree of life. A phylogeny is any piece of that tree. A species is, roughly, one twig: a group that breeds among itself. The three biggest branches are called domains: Bacteria, Archaea (which look like bacteria but are chemically a different lineage), and Eukarya (everything with a nucleus in its cells, including plants, fungi, animals, and you).

Picture
The technical version

The tree of life is the phylogeny of all organisms, inferred largely from comparing gene sequences (originally ribosomal RNA, by Woese). Its three domains are Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. Bacteria and Archaea are prokaryotic (no nucleus); Eukarya are eukaryotic. A species is commonly defined as a group of interbreeding populations reproductively isolated from others, though the course will show that this definition has edges.

Prokaryote and eukaryote

In plain words

Two ways to build a cell. A prokaryote keeps its DNA loose in the main compartment, like a studio apartment: one room, everything in it. A eukaryote keeps its DNA inside a walled-off inner room, the nucleus, and has other little rooms with jobs, like a house. Bacteria and Archaea are prokaryotes. Everything else, including every cell in your body, is a eukaryote. The prefix tells you: pro- "before", eu- "true", karyon "kernel", so "before a kernel" versus "true kernel".

Picture
The technical version

Prokaryotic cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus; their chromosome sits in a region of cytoplasm called the nucleoid, and they generally lack membrane-bound organelles. Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus enclosed by a double membrane and a set of membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi, and so on). Eukaryotic cells are typically much larger. Chapter 7 details both.

Hypothesis, prediction, experiment, control

In plain words

A hypothesis is a proposed explanation you can test: "the plant died because it got too little light." A prediction is what you should see if the hypothesis is right: "plants in the shade will grow less than plants in the sun." The experiment is the setup that checks the prediction. The control is the comparison group where you change nothing, so that if the shaded plants grow less you can say it was the shade and not the pot or the water. Without a control you can't tell what caused what.

Picture
The technical version

A hypothesis is a testable explanation for an observation; a prediction is a measurable outcome that must hold if the hypothesis is correct. A controlled experiment varies one factor (the independent variable) between a treatment group and a control group, holds other conditions constant, and measures the response (the dependent variable). Replicates and controls let you distinguish an effect from chance. Freeman uses the null hypothesis, the "no effect" alternative, as the comparison.

Theoryand why it doesn't mean "guess"

In plain words

In everyday English a theory is a hunch. In science it is the opposite: an explanation that has survived so many tests that it organizes a whole field. Cell theory and the theory of evolution are theories in that sense, the same sense as the theory of gravity. A hypothesis is one testable guess; a theory is a big, well-tested explanation that many hypotheses hang from. The word trap is real and it shows up on exams.

Picture
Hold this image: a hypothesis is a single brick you are testing to see if it holds weight. A theory is a building that has stood for a century, made of thousands of bricks that all held. Nobody calls the building "just a brick."
The technical version

A scientific theory is a broad explanatory framework supported by a large body of evidence and capable of generating testable hypotheses. It is not provisional in the everyday sense; it can be refined but is not overturned by a single result. Contrast: hypothesis (specific, testable, provisional) and law (a description of a regularity, not an explanation).

Chapter 2Water and Carbon: The Chemical Basis of Life

The forest

This chapter is the chemistry the course assumes you had. The whole thing comes down to one idea: atoms stick to each other in a few different ways, and the way they stick decides everything else. Atoms share electrons (a covalent bond), or one steals from another (an ionic bond). When two atoms share unevenly, the molecule gets a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end (it is polar), and polar molecules are attracted to each other (that attraction is a hydrogen bond). Water is polar, which is why it clings to itself, dissolves things, and resists changing temperature. Every property of water in the chapter is that one fact wearing a different hat.

Carbon gets the other half of the chapter because a carbon atom can make four bonds, so it can build chains and rings, so it can build the big molecules of Chapters 3 through 6. The functional groups are the little attachments on those carbon skeletons that give each molecule its personality.

One sentence: uneven sharing of electrons makes water sticky; carbon's four bonds make big molecules possible.

Atomproton · neutron · electron · element

In plain words

An atom is the smallest piece of a substance that is still that substance. It has a heavy center (protons, which are positive, and neutrons, which are neutral) and a cloud of much lighter electrons (negative) around it. The number of protons decides which element it is: 6 protons is always carbon, 8 is always oxygen. Electrons are the part that does chemistry, because they are on the outside and can be shared or handed over.

Picture
The technical version

An atom consists of a nucleus of protons (charge +1) and neutrons (charge 0), surrounded by electrons (charge −1) occupying orbitals grouped into shells. Atomic number = number of protons and defines the element; mass number = protons + neutrons. Atoms of the same element with different neutron numbers are isotopes. Chemical behaviour is determined by the electrons in the outermost (valence) shell.

Valenceelectron shells · why atoms bond at all

In plain words

Electrons sit in layers (shells) around the nucleus, and each layer has a fixed number of seats. Atoms are "happiest" when the outer layer is full. Most of them aren't, so they bond with other atoms to share or swap electrons until the outer layer fills up. Valence is the number of bonds an atom makes to get there: hydrogen 1, oxygen 2, nitrogen 3, carbon 4. That single number explains the shape of every molecule in the course.

Picture
The technical version

Electrons occupy shells of increasing energy; the first holds 2, the second 8. Atoms are most stable with a filled valence (outermost) shell, and the number of unpaired valence electrons sets an atom's valence, the number of covalent bonds it typically forms: H 1, O 2, N 3, C 4. Unpaired valence electrons are what bonding pairs up.

Covalent bondmolecule · nonpolar vs polar

In plain words

Two atoms both need an electron, so they share a pair between them. That shared pair is a covalent bond, and it is strong: it holds molecules together. A molecule is just atoms held together by covalent bonds. If the two atoms pull on the shared pair equally, the bond is nonpolar (like two people carrying a box evenly). If one atom pulls harder, the electrons spend more time near it, so that end goes slightly negative and the other end slightly positive: the bond is polar. Oxygen and nitrogen pull hard; carbon and hydrogen pull about the same as each other.

Picture
The technical version

A covalent bond is a shared pair of valence electrons between two atoms; a molecule is two or more atoms joined covalently. Electronegativity is an atom's tendency to attract shared electrons (O and N high; C and H similar and moderate). Equal sharing gives a nonpolar covalent bond (C–H, C–C); unequal sharing gives a polar covalent bond (O–H, N–H) with partial charges δ− on the more electronegative atom and δ+ on the other. Double and triple bonds share two or three pairs.

Ionic bondion · cation · anion

In plain words

Sometimes one atom doesn't share; it takes an electron outright. The taker ends up with an extra negative charge, the giver with a missing one, so now one is negative and one is positive, and opposite charges attract. That attraction is an ionic bond. An atom with a charge is an ion: a positive one is a cation, a negative one an anion. Table salt is sodium (gave one away, now +) stuck to chloride (took it, now −). In water, ionic bonds come apart easily, which is why salt dissolves.

Picture
The technical version

An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed when one atom transfers one or more electrons to another. Cations carry net positive charge (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺); anions net negative (Cl⁻). In aqueous solution the ions are surrounded by water molecules (hydration shells) and dissociate. Many biologically important ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, H⁺) appear throughout the course.

Hydrogen bond

In plain words

This is the one the chapter turns on, and the name is misleading: it is not a bond inside a molecule. It is a weak attraction between molecules. When a hydrogen is attached to an oxygen or nitrogen, it is left slightly positive (the O or N hogs the electrons). That slightly positive hydrogen is attracted to a slightly negative oxygen or nitrogen on a neighbouring molecule. One hydrogen bond is weak, like a Velcro hook. Millions of them together are strong, like a whole strip of Velcro. Water sticks to itself with them; DNA's two strands are held together with them; proteins hold their shape with them.

Picture
The technical version

A hydrogen bond is a weak electrostatic attraction between a partially positive hydrogen atom that is covalently bonded to an electronegative atom (O or N) and a partially negative O or N on another molecule or another part of the same molecule. Individually weak (about 1/20 of a covalent bond) and constantly breaking and re-forming, hydrogen bonds are collectively responsible for water's cohesion, the pairing of DNA strands, and protein secondary structure.

Water's propertiescohesion · adhesion · surface tension · specific heat

In plain words

Every odd thing water does is hydrogen bonding wearing a different hat. Water molecules cling to each other (cohesion), which is why a drop holds together and why the surface acts like a stretched skin (surface tension). They also cling to other polar surfaces (adhesion), which is why water creeps up a paper towel. It takes a lot of heat to warm water up, because the heat first has to shake the hydrogen bonds loose (high specific heat), which is why oceans and your body resist temperature swings. And ice floats because the hydrogen bonds lock the molecules into an open lattice that is less dense than the liquid.

Picture
The technical version

Cohesion: attraction between like molecules, here water to water via hydrogen bonding, producing surface tension. Adhesion: attraction between unlike molecules, e.g. water to polar surfaces. Specific heat: energy required to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 °C; water's is high because energy goes into breaking hydrogen bonds before raising kinetic energy. Heat of vaporization is high for the same reason (evaporative cooling). Ice is less dense than liquid water because hydrogen bonds hold molecules in an open crystal lattice. Water is also an excellent solvent for polar and charged solutes.

Hydrophilic and hydrophobicsolvent · solute · solution

In plain words

A solvent is the liquid doing the dissolving; a solute is the thing dissolved in it; together they are a solution. In biology the solvent is almost always water. Water dissolves things that are polar or charged, because its own partial charges can surround them and pull them apart; such things are hydrophilic, "water-loving": salt, sugar, most of what's in a cell. Things that are nonpolar, like oil and fat, have no charges for water to grab, so water molecules would rather hold hands with each other and the oil gets pushed aside; those are hydrophobic, "water-fearing". Hydrophobic doesn't mean the oil is afraid; it means water excludes it. This one idea builds cell membranes in Chapter 6 and folds proteins in Chapter 3.

Picture
The technical version

Hydrophilic substances are polar or ionic and interact favourably with water, dissolving as water molecules form hydration shells around them. Hydrophobic substances are nonpolar and do not interact with water; they aggregate because that arrangement lets water maximise its own hydrogen bonding (the hydrophobic effect, an entropy-driven process). A solution is a homogeneous mixture of solute in solvent; concentration is often given in molarity (moles of solute per litre).

Acid, base, and pHbuffer

In plain words

Water molecules occasionally split into a hydrogen ion (H⁺, a bare proton) and a hydroxide ion (OH⁻). An acid is anything that adds H⁺ to a solution; a base is anything that removes it. pH is a scale for how much H⁺ there is: 7 is neutral (pure water), lower is more acidic, higher is more basic. It runs backwards and in powers of ten: pH 3 has ten times more H⁺ than pH 4, and a thousand times more than pH 6. Your blood is held at about 7.4 by buffers, molecules that soak up or release H⁺ to stop the number drifting, because proteins fall apart if it does.

Picture
The technical version

Water dissociates slightly: H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻ (the proton is actually carried as hydronium, H₃O⁺). An acid donates protons, raising [H⁺]; a base accepts protons, lowering it. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; at 25 °C pure water has [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷ M, so pH 7. A buffer is a weak acid/conjugate base pair that minimises pH change by absorbing or releasing H⁺; the carbonic acid/bicarbonate system buffers blood near 7.4.

Carbon and organic moleculescarbon skeleton · hydrocarbon

In plain words

"Organic" in chemistry has nothing to do with farming. It means "contains carbon bonded to hydrogen." Carbon is the building material of life because it makes four bonds, so carbon atoms can link into long chains, branches and rings, and hang other atoms off the sides. That chain or ring is the carbon skeleton; the molecule is built on it. A hydrocarbon is a skeleton with only hydrogens attached, like the stuff in gasoline; it is nonpolar, so it is hydrophobic. Add oxygen or nitrogen somewhere and the molecule gets a polar spot and a personality.

Picture
The technical version

Organic molecules contain carbon covalently bonded to hydrogen. Carbon's valence of four and its ability to form single, double and triple bonds with itself and with H, O, N, S and P allow diverse skeletons: linear, branched, and ring structures. Hydrocarbons are nonpolar. The chapter also introduces Freeman's theme of chemical evolution: the hypothesis that simple carbon compounds formed and grew more complex under early-Earth conditions before life existed.

Functional groups

In plain words

A functional group is a small cluster of atoms that hangs off a carbon skeleton and gives the molecule a specific behaviour. Think of the skeleton as a phone and the functional groups as the apps: the phone does nothing by itself, the apps make it useful. There are six the course cares about. A hydroxyl (–OH) makes a spot polar and water-friendly. A carboxyl (–COOH) acts as an acid. An amino (–NH₂) acts as a base. A phosphate (–OPO₃²⁻) carries negative charge and energy. A carbonyl (C=O) is a reactive spot found in sugars. A sulfhydryl (–SH) can lock onto another sulfhydryl to pin a protein's shape.

Picture
The technical version

Functional groups are specific groups of atoms with characteristic chemical properties regardless of the skeleton they attach to. Hydroxyl (–OH): polar, hydrogen-bonds. Carbonyl (C=O): aldehyde if terminal, ketone if internal; reactive. Carboxyl (–COOH): weak acid, ionises to –COO⁻. Amino (–NH₂): weak base, protonates to –NH₃⁺. Phosphate (–OPO₃²⁻): negatively charged; links nucleotides and stores energy in ATP. Sulfhydryl (–SH): forms disulfide bridges (S–S) between cysteines in proteins. Methyl (–CH₃) is nonpolar and appears in later chapters as a regulatory tag.

Chemical reaction and energyreactants · products · potential energy · spontaneous

In plain words

A chemical reaction is atoms rearranging: bonds break, different bonds form. What you start with are the reactants, what you end with are the products. Energy is stored in bonds the way energy is stored in a stretched spring (that is potential energy), and different bonds store different amounts, so a reaction either releases energy or needs energy put in. A reaction that runs on its own, releasing energy and usually making things more disordered, is called spontaneous. "Spontaneous" says nothing about speed: rusting is spontaneous and takes years. The chapter's deeper point is that the universe drifts toward lower energy and more disorder, and life is the trick of using energy flow to build order anyway.

Picture
The technical version

In a chemical reaction, reactants are converted to products by breaking and forming covalent bonds. Potential energy is stored in chemical bonds; kinetic energy is energy of motion (including thermal energy). Freeman introduces Gibbs free energy: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, where ΔH is change in enthalpy (heat content) and ΔS change in entropy (disorder). Reactions with ΔG < 0 are exergonic and spontaneous; ΔG > 0 endergonic, requiring energy input. The second law of thermodynamics states that total entropy increases. Spontaneity is independent of rate, which is set by activation energy (Chapter 3, enzymes).

Chapter 3Protein Structure and Function

The forest

Proteins are the machines of the cell: the things that do stuff. They carry oxygen, cut and paste other molecules, form muscle, recognise invaders, run every chemical reaction in you. The whole chapter is one idea told at four levels: a protein is a chain of small units (amino acids), and the order of the units decides how the chain folds, and the fold decides what the protein can do. Shape is function. Change the shape, whether by heat, acid, or one wrong unit, and the machine stops working.

The second half of the chapter is about the most important job proteins do: acting as enzymes, which make specific reactions happen millions of times faster by holding the reacting molecules in exactly the right position. An enzyme doesn't add energy; it lowers the hill.

One sentence: twenty kinds of bead, strung in an order, fold into a shape, and the shape is the job.

Biomolecules · Enzymes

Monomer and polymercondensation (dehydration) reaction · hydrolysis

In plain words

A monomer is one bead; a polymer is a string of beads. Proteins, DNA, and starch are all polymers, each made from its own kind of bead. The cell links two beads by pulling an H off one and an OH off the other and joining what's left; the H and OH leave together as a water molecule. Because a water is removed, the joining reaction is called a condensation or dehydration reaction. Taking the string apart is the reverse: put a water back in and the link breaks. That is hydrolysis ("water-splitting"), which is what digestion is.

Picture
The technical version

Monomers are small molecular subunits; polymers are chains of monomers joined by covalent bonds. Polymerisation proceeds by condensation (dehydration) reactions, in which a hydroxyl from one monomer and a hydrogen from another are removed as water and a covalent bond forms. Hydrolysis reverses this by adding water. Condensation is endergonic and in cells is coupled to energy sources (ATP); hydrolysis is exergonic. The specific bonds are peptide bonds (proteins), phosphodiester bonds (nucleic acids), and glycosidic linkages (carbohydrates).

Amino acidR-group (side chain)

In plain words

The bead that proteins are made of. Every amino acid has the same core: a central carbon with an amino group on one side, a carboxyl group on the other, a hydrogen, and one variable part called the R-group or side chain. The twenty amino acids differ only in that side chain. Some side chains are oily (hydrophobic), some are polar, some carry a charge. That variety is the point: it's what lets a chain fold into a specific shape, because the oily ones hide inside and the polar ones face the water.

Picture
The technical version

An amino acid has a central α-carbon bonded to an amino group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen, and a side chain (R-group). At cellular pH the amino group is protonated (–NH₃⁺) and the carboxyl deprotonated (–COO⁻). The 20 standard amino acids are grouped by R-group chemistry: nonpolar (hydrophobic), polar uncharged, and charged (acidic or basic). Side-chain properties drive folding and determine function.

Peptide bondpolypeptide · protein

In plain words

The link between two amino acids. The carboxyl end of one joins the amino end of the next, a water leaves, and the bond that forms is a peptide bond. String enough together and you have a polypeptide: a chain with a definite direction, an amino end and a carboxyl end. A protein is one or more polypeptides folded into a working shape. Chains are anywhere from a few dozen to thousands of amino acids long.

Picture
The technical version

A peptide bond is the covalent C–N bond formed by condensation between the carboxyl carbon of one amino acid and the amino nitrogen of the next. It has partial double-bond character, so it is planar and rigid, which constrains folding. A polypeptide has an N-terminus and a C-terminus; the repeating N–Cα–C atoms form the backbone, and R-groups project from it. Proteins are functional polypeptides; oligopeptides are short chains.

The four levels of protein structureprimary · secondary (α-helix, β-sheet) · tertiary · quaternary

In plain words

Four ways to describe the same protein, zooming out. Primary: the order of the beads, like the letters in a word. Secondary: short stretches of the chain that coil into a spiral (an alpha helix) or fold back and forth into a flat pleat (a beta sheet), held by hydrogen bonds along the backbone. Tertiary: the whole chain folded up into its 3-D shape, held mostly by the side chains, with oily ones tucked inside away from water. Quaternary: some proteins are several folded chains clicked together; that arrangement is the fourth level. Not every protein has a quaternary level; every protein has the first three.

Picture
The technical version

Primary structure: the amino acid sequence, joined by peptide bonds. Secondary structure: regular local patterns, the α-helix and β-pleated sheet, stabilised by hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H groups. Tertiary structure: the overall three-dimensional fold of one polypeptide, stabilised by side-chain interactions: hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, van der Waals interactions, and covalent disulfide bridges. Quaternary structure: the association of two or more polypeptide subunits (e.g. haemoglobin, four subunits). Sequence determines structure; structure determines function.

Folding, shape, and denaturation

In plain words

A chain of amino acids does not stay a floppy string. In water it folds itself, in a fraction of a second, into one particular shape, because its oily side chains huddle away from water and its polar ones reach toward it. That shape is what does the job: a pocket that fits one molecule, a groove that grips DNA, a hinge that opens. Heat, acid, or the wrong chemical can shake the folds apart; that is denaturation, and a denatured protein has stopped working even though every bead is still there. A cooked egg white is denatured protein: same molecules, wrong shape, no going back.

Picture
The technical version

Folding is spontaneous and driven largely by the hydrophobic effect, with the final conformation stabilised by the interactions listed under tertiary structure; chaperone proteins assist folding in cells. Denaturation is loss of native conformation, and therefore function, without breaking peptide bonds, caused by heat, pH change, or chemicals that disrupt non-covalent interactions. It can be reversible for small proteins (Anfinsen's ribonuclease experiment showed sequence alone specifies the fold) but is often irreversible in practice. Misfolded proteins that propagate their shape are prions.

Enzymecatalyst · substrate · active site · activation energy

In plain words

An enzyme is a protein whose job is to make one specific reaction happen fast. Most reactions in a cell would take years on their own, not because they're uphill but because the molecules need a jolt to get started, the way a match needs striking. That starting jolt is the activation energy. An enzyme has a pocket (the active site) shaped to hold the reacting molecule (the substrate) in exactly the position where the reaction is easiest, so the jolt needed is much smaller and the reaction runs millions of times faster. The enzyme itself is unchanged afterwards and goes again; that is what "catalyst" means. Each enzyme fits one substrate, which is why the cell needs thousands of them.

Picture
The technical version

A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction without being consumed and without changing ΔG. Enzymes are protein catalysts (some RNAs also catalyse; see ribozyme). The substrate binds the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex; binding often involves induced fit, a conformational change that tightens the fit. The enzyme lowers activation energy (E_a) by stabilising the transition state, orienting substrates, and providing a favourable microenvironment. Rate depends on substrate concentration, temperature and pH; many enzymes need cofactors (metal ions) or coenzymes (organic, often vitamin-derived). Regulation by inhibitors and feedback comes later in the course.

Chapter 4Nucleic Acids and an RNA World

The forest

DNA is a set of instructions written in a four-letter alphabet, and the chapter is about the alphabet's physical form. Each letter is a nucleotide: a sugar, a phosphate, and one of four bases. Nucleotides link into a strand with a backbone of sugar-phosphate-sugar-phosphate and the bases sticking out like the teeth of a zipper. Two strands zip together because each base has one partner it hydrogen-bonds with: A with T, G with C. That pairing rule is the whole reason DNA can be copied: pull the strands apart and each one dictates its partner.

RNA is DNA's single-stranded cousin, with one different sugar and one different letter (U instead of T). Because it is single-stranded it can fold up on itself into shapes, and shapes can do chemistry, so RNA can act as an enzyme. The "RNA world" is the idea that before there were proteins or DNA, RNA did both jobs, storing information and catalysing reactions. That is Freeman's hypothesis for how life got started, and it explains why this chapter comes before the cell.

One sentence: four letters on a sugar-phosphate string, paired A–T and G–C, so the message copies itself.

Nucleotidephosphate · sugar · nitrogenous base

In plain words

The bead that DNA and RNA are made of, and it has three parts stuck together: a phosphate group (the charged, energy-carrying attachment from Chapter 2), a five-carbon sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), and a base, which is the "letter" part, a flat ring containing nitrogen. Sugar and phosphate are the same in every bead of a strand; only the base changes. So a strand is a monotonous backbone with a message written on the side.

Picture
The technical version

A nucleotide consists of a phosphate group, a pentose sugar (ribose in RNA; 2′-deoxyribose in DNA, lacking the 2′ –OH), and a nitrogenous base attached at the sugar's 1′ carbon. The phosphate is attached at 5′; the 3′ carbon carries the hydroxyl that forms the next linkage. Sugar carbons are numbered with primes to distinguish them from base atoms. A nucleoside is sugar plus base without phosphate. Nucleoside triphosphates (ATP, GTP) are the activated monomers used for polymerisation and, in ATP's case, the cell's energy currency.

The basesA, T, G, C, U · purine vs pyrimidine · complementary pairing

In plain words

Four letters in DNA: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), cytosine (C). RNA swaps T for uracil (U). They come in two sizes: A and G are big (two rings, called purines); T, C and U are small (one ring, pyrimidines). The pairing rule follows from the sizes and from where their hydrogen-bond spots are: a big one always pairs with a small one, and specifically A with T (two hydrogen bonds) and G with C (three). "Complementary" means "the matching partner": if one strand reads ATGC, its partner reads TACG. That is the rule that makes copying possible.

Picture
The technical version

Purines (adenine, guanine) are double-ring bases; pyrimidines (cytosine, thymine, uracil) are single-ring. Complementary base pairing via hydrogen bonds: A pairs with T (or U in RNA) by two hydrogen bonds; G pairs with C by three. Purine–pyrimidine pairing keeps the double helix at constant diameter (Chargaff's rules: [A]=[T], [G]=[C]). GC-rich regions are more thermally stable. Uracil replaces thymine in RNA.

Backbone and directionphosphodiester bond · sugar-phosphate backbone · 5′ and 3′ ends · antiparallel

In plain words

Beads link handle to handle: the phosphate of one nucleotide bonds to the sugar of the next, so the strand's spine is sugar-phosphate-sugar-phosphate all the way along, with bases sticking out sideways. That link is a phosphodiester bond. Because each sugar has its phosphate on one corner (called 5′, "five-prime") and its free hook on another (3′), a strand has a direction: it starts at a 5′ end and finishes at a 3′ end, like a sentence read left to right. In double-stranded DNA the two strands run in opposite directions, one 5′→3′ and the other 3′→5′; that is what "antiparallel" means. Chapters 15 to 17 depend on this, because the copying machinery can only build in one direction.

Picture
The technical version

A phosphodiester bond links the 3′ hydroxyl of one nucleotide's sugar to the 5′ phosphate of the next, forming a directional sugar-phosphate backbone with a free 5′ phosphate at one end and a free 3′ hydroxyl at the other. Sequences are written 5′→3′. Polymerases add nucleotides only to the 3′ end, so strands grow 5′→3′. In DNA the two strands are antiparallel and complementary, wound into a right-handed double helix (Watson and Crick, 1953) with the bases stacked inside and the backbones outside; the helix has a major and a minor groove where proteins read the sequence.

DNA vs RNAdouble helix · template · gene

In plain words

DNA is two strands zipped into a twisted ladder (the double helix): sturdy, stable, good for long-term storage of the instructions. RNA is one strand, with a slightly different sugar (ribose, with one extra oxygen) and U in place of T. Being single, RNA can fold back on itself and pair with its own bases, making shapes, which lets it do jobs rather than just store text. The relationship is: DNA is the master copy kept in the archive; RNA is the working copy that gets sent out. A gene is a stretch of DNA that holds the instructions for one product, usually a protein. Because each strand pairs only with its complement, either strand can serve as a template for building the other, which is how DNA copies itself and how RNA gets made from it.

Picture
The technical version

DNA: 2′-deoxyribose, bases A T G C, double-stranded antiparallel helix; chemically stable (the missing 2′ –OH makes it resistant to hydrolysis), which suits information storage. RNA: ribose, bases A U G C, usually single-stranded but with extensive intramolecular base pairing producing secondary structure (hairpins, stem-loops) and tertiary structure; more reactive, and functional as messenger, structural, and catalytic molecules. A template strand directs synthesis of a complementary strand by base pairing; this underlies replication (Chapter 15) and transcription (Chapter 17). A gene is a DNA sequence encoding a functional product, usually a polypeptide or an RNA.

RNA worldribozyme · chemical evolution

In plain words

A chicken-and-egg problem: proteins are needed to copy DNA, but DNA is needed to make proteins. So which came first? The RNA-world hypothesis says neither. RNA can store information (it has bases that pair) and RNA can fold into shapes that do chemistry (an RNA enzyme is a ribozyme), so early life could have run on RNA alone, with DNA and proteins arriving later as specialists. The evidence includes ribozymes still working in your cells today: the machine that builds every protein in you, the ribosome, is at its core an RNA catalyst. Chemical evolution is Freeman's broader term for the whole idea that simple molecules on the early Earth grew more complex, step by step, until something could copy itself.

Picture
Hold this image: a recipe card that can also cook. DNA is only a recipe; a protein is only a cook; RNA, folded up, can be both, badly. "Badly" is enough to get started, and then the specialists take over.
The technical version

The RNA-world hypothesis proposes that self-replicating RNA molecules preceded DNA and proteins as the first information-carrying and catalytic molecules. Support: RNA's capacity for complementary base pairing (information) and for folding into catalytic structures (ribozymes, discovered by Cech and Altman); in vitro selection of RNAs that catalyse their own partial replication; and the peptidyl transferase activity of the ribosome residing in rRNA. Chemical evolution refers to the abiotic formation of increasingly complex carbon compounds, including nucleotides, under early-Earth conditions, followed by polymerisation.

Chapter 5An Introduction to Carbohydrates

The forest

Carbohydrates are sugars and the things built from sugars. The chapter is short and it is one idea: a sugar is a small ring of carbons, sugars link into chains, and the same sugar linked two slightly different ways gives you either food or wood. Starch (in potatoes) and cellulose (in plant cell walls, paper, cotton) are both long chains of glucose. The only difference is the angle of the link between the beads, and that difference decides whether your enzymes can cut it. You digest starch. You cannot digest cellulose. Same beads.

Carbohydrates do three jobs: they store energy (starch in plants, glycogen in you), they build structures (cellulose, chitin), and, attached to proteins on the outside of cells, they act as identity tags that other cells read.

One sentence: rings of glucose, chained; the link angle decides whether it's fuel or scaffolding.

Biomolecules

Sugarcarbohydrate · monosaccharide · glucose

In plain words

A carbohydrate is a molecule made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen in roughly the proportions of carbon plus water, which is what the name means: carbo-hydrate. The simplest carbohydrate is a single sugar, a monosaccharide, and the one that matters most is glucose: six carbons that curl into a ring in water, with an –OH hanging off most of them. All those –OH groups make sugars very polar, so they dissolve easily and are sticky with hydrogen bonds. Glucose is the fuel nearly every cell on Earth burns, and Chapter 9 is about how.

Picture
The technical version

Carbohydrates have the general formula (CH₂O)ₙ and are defined by a carbonyl group (aldehyde or ketone) plus multiple hydroxyl groups. Monosaccharides are classified by carbon number (trioses, pentoses like ribose, hexoses like glucose) and by carbonyl position (aldose vs ketose). In solution, five- and six-carbon sugars cyclise; the ring form of glucose exists as α and β anomers, differing only in the orientation of the –OH on carbon 1, and that difference is what distinguishes starch from cellulose. Glucose, galactose and fructose are isomers: same formula, different arrangement.

Linking sugarsglycosidic linkage · disaccharide · polysaccharide

In plain words

Sugars join the same way amino acids do: an –OH from each, a water leaves, a bond forms. Between sugars that bond is called a glycosidic linkage. Two sugars joined make a disaccharide: table sugar (sucrose) is glucose plus fructose; milk sugar (lactose) is glucose plus galactose. Many sugars joined make a polysaccharide, and those are the big ones: starch, glycogen, cellulose, chitin. Unlike proteins, where twenty different beads make an alphabet, most polysaccharides are one bead repeated. What varies is the linkage and the branching.

Picture
The technical version

A glycosidic linkage is the covalent bond formed by a condensation reaction between hydroxyl groups of two monosaccharides. Linkages are named by the carbons joined and the anomer of the first sugar: α-1,4 in maltose and starch, β-1,4 in cellulose, α-1,2 in sucrose. Disaccharides: sucrose (glucose + fructose), lactose (glucose + galactose), maltose (glucose + glucose). Polysaccharides may be linear or branched (α-1,6 branch points in glycogen and amylopectin).

Starch, glycogen, cellulose, chitinα versus β linkage · why you can't digest wood

In plain words

Four famous polysaccharides. Starch is how plants store glucose; glycogen is how animals do, mostly in liver and muscle; both are glucose chains with the link at one angle (called alpha), which makes the chain coil up like a spring, and your enzymes can cut it. Cellulose is also a glucose chain, but every other bead is flipped so the link is at the other angle (beta); that makes the chain dead straight, and straight chains stack into fibres held by hydrogen bonds. That is wood, cotton and paper. No enzyme in your body can cut a beta link, so cellulose passes through you as fibre. Chitin is the same trick with a slightly modified sugar, and it makes insect shells and fungal walls.

Picture
The technical version

Starch (plants) is a mixture of amylose (unbranched α-1,4 glucose, helical) and amylopectin (α-1,4 with α-1,6 branches). Glycogen (animals) is α-1,4 glucose with more frequent α-1,6 branching, stored in liver and muscle. Cellulose is unbranched β-1,4 glucose; alternate residues are flipped 180°, giving a linear chain that hydrogen-bonds to neighbouring chains into microfibrils. Chitin is β-1,4 N-acetylglucosamine (exoskeletons, fungal walls); peptidoglycan is a related bacterial wall polymer. Humans lack cellulase; ruminants and termites rely on symbiotic microbes that have it.

What carbohydrates doenergy · structure · cell identity (glycoproteins)

In plain words

Three jobs. Energy: glucose is burned for power, and starch and glycogen are the pantry it's kept in. Structure: cellulose and chitin are load-bearing. Identity: short sugar chains stuck onto proteins on the outer surface of a cell act as name tags. Your blood type is a sugar tag; your immune system decides what is "you" partly by reading sugar tags. A protein with sugars attached is a glycoprotein.

Picture
Hold this image: a house. The firewood in the shed is starch. The timber frame is cellulose. The nameplate by the door is the sugar tag on a glycoprotein. Same material, three jobs.
The technical version

Carbohydrates serve as energy stores (starch, glycogen; glucose oxidation in cellular respiration yields ATP), structural polymers (cellulose, chitin, peptidoglycan), and as recognition molecules. Glycoproteins and glycolipids on the extracellular face of the plasma membrane carry oligosaccharides that mediate cell–cell recognition and signalling; ABO blood groups differ by the terminal sugar on a membrane glycolipid. Carbohydrates also form the backbone of nucleic acids (ribose, deoxyribose).

Chapter 6Lipids, Membranes, and the First Cells

The forest

This chapter takes one property from Chapter 2, that oil and water don't mix, and builds a cell out of it. Lipids are the oily molecules. A phospholipid is a lipid with a water-loving head and two water-fearing tails, and when you drop a pile of them in water they arrange themselves, with no help, into a two-layer sheet with the tails hidden inside. That sheet is a membrane. It closes into a bubble. The bubble has an inside and an outside, and that is the beginning of a cell.

The second half is about what gets through the sheet. Small uncharged things slip through; ions and big polar things can't, unless a protein lets them. Diffusion is things spreading out; osmosis is water spreading toward the saltier side; and the proteins in the membrane are the doors, some open, some needing a push (energy) to move things uphill.

One sentence: oily tails hide from water, so phospholipids self-assemble into a bag; proteins in the bag decide what gets in.

Lipidfatty acid · saturated vs unsaturated

In plain words

"Lipid" is the chemist's word for fats, oils, and their relatives: molecules that are mostly carbon and hydrogen, so they are nonpolar and won't dissolve in water. Unlike proteins and sugars, lipids aren't a chain of one repeating bead; they're a family defined by that one behaviour. The basic building block is the fatty acid: a long tail of carbons and hydrogens with an acid group at one end. If every carbon in the tail holds as many hydrogens as it can, the tail is straight and the fat is "saturated" (butter, solid at room temperature). If there's a double bond somewhere, the tail has a kink, the molecules can't pack tightly, and the fat is "unsaturated" (olive oil, liquid).

Picture
The technical version

Lipids are defined functionally as carbon-containing compounds that are largely nonpolar and hydrophobic; they are not polymers. A fatty acid is a hydrocarbon chain (typically 12–22 carbons) with a terminal carboxyl group. Saturated fatty acids have only C–C single bonds; unsaturated have one or more C=C double bonds, which in the cis configuration introduce a bend that lowers packing density and melting point. Trans fats are unsaturated but straight. Degree of saturation and chain length control membrane fluidity.

Fat and phospholipidtriglyceride · glycerol · amphipathic · steroid

In plain words

A fat (triglyceride) is three fatty-acid tails bolted onto a small three-carbon molecule called glycerol. It is entirely oily, which is why it's good for storing energy and useless for building membranes. A phospholipid is the same idea with one tail swapped for a phosphate group, and that changes everything: the phosphate end is charged and loves water, while the two remaining tails hate it. A molecule with a water-loving part and a water-fearing part is called amphipathic, and amphipathic molecules are what membranes are made of. Steroids are lipids of a different shape, four fused rings; cholesterol is one, and it sits in membranes to tune their stiffness.

Picture
The technical version

Triglycerides (fats): glycerol esterified to three fatty acids via ester linkages formed by condensation; nonpolar, energy-dense storage. Phospholipids: glycerol with two fatty acids and a phosphate group (often bearing a further polar group such as choline); the phosphate head is hydrophilic and the tails hydrophobic, making the molecule amphipathic. Steroids: four fused carbon rings; cholesterol is amphipathic (a single –OH) and modulates membrane fluidity in animal cells. Isoprenoids and waxes are other lipid classes.

Lipid bilayerplasma membrane · fluid mosaic model · micelle

In plain words

Drop phospholipids into water and they sort themselves out: heads toward the water, tails away from it. The stable arrangement is two layers back to back, tails in the middle where there's no water, heads facing out on both sides. That is a lipid bilayer, and it is what every cell membrane is. Nobody builds it; it assembles because the tails are hiding. It is also fluid: the molecules slide around each other like people in a crowd, and proteins float in it like boats. "Fluid mosaic" is the name for that picture: a moving sea of lipids with a mosaic of proteins in it. A micelle is the one-layer version, a ball with tails pointing to the centre, which forms when the lipids have only one tail.

Picture
The technical version

Amphipathic phospholipids in water spontaneously form bilayers (two tails) or micelles (one tail) to minimise hydrophobic exposure; the hydrophobic effect drives assembly. The fluid mosaic model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972) describes the membrane as a fluid phospholipid bilayer with integral (transmembrane) and peripheral proteins. Fluidity increases with unsaturation and temperature and is buffered by cholesterol. Lipids diffuse laterally rapidly but rarely flip between leaflets. Freeman's "first cells" thread: bilayer vesicles form abiotically, offering a plausible origin of the first compartments.

Selective permeability

In plain words

The bilayer is a wall with rules. Small molecules with no charge, like oxygen, carbon dioxide and water (a little), can squeeze through the oily middle. Anything charged (ions like Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) or large and polar (sugars, amino acids) cannot: the oily middle rejects them exactly the way oil rejects salt water. "Selectively permeable" means it lets some things through and not others, and the things it blocks are precisely the ones the cell most needs to control. That is why the membrane is full of proteins: they are the exceptions to the rule.

Picture
The technical version

Permeability of a pure bilayer scales with lipid solubility and inversely with size: small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, N₂) cross rapidly; small uncharged polar molecules (H₂O, glycerol) slowly; large polar molecules (glucose) and all ions essentially not at all. Membrane proteins (channels, carriers, pumps) confer selective permeability to those solutes. Water crossing is greatly accelerated by aquaporin channels.

Diffusionconcentration gradient · equilibrium

In plain words

Molecules in a liquid or gas are always jiggling randomly. If there are more of them on one side of a room than the other, more will happen to jiggle from the crowded side to the empty side than the reverse, so on average they spread out until they're even. That spreading is diffusion. Nothing pushes them; it's just that random motion from a crowded place ends up somewhere less crowded more often than not. A concentration gradient is the difference between the crowded side and the empty side, and things diffuse "down" it, from more to less. When it's even, diffusion hasn't stopped, the molecules still move; there's just no net change. That is equilibrium.

Picture
The technical version

Diffusion is net movement of molecules from higher to lower concentration driven by random thermal motion; it is a spontaneous process that increases entropy and requires no energy input. A concentration gradient is the difference in concentration across a distance. Net diffusion ceases at equilibrium, when concentrations are equal, though individual molecules continue to move. Passive transport across membranes (simple and facilitated diffusion) is diffusion down a gradient; for ions, the electrical gradient also matters (electrochemical gradient).

Osmosishypertonic · hypotonic · isotonic

In plain words

Osmosis is diffusion of water, specifically across a membrane that lets water through but not the stuff dissolved in it. Water moves toward the side with more dissolved stuff (the saltier side), because that side has proportionally less water. The three words describe the outside relative to the inside of a cell. Hypertonic: the outside is saltier, water leaves, the cell shrivels. Hypotonic: the outside is more dilute, water rushes in, the cell swells and can burst. Isotonic: equal, no net change. The prefixes help: hyper- "more", hypo- "less", iso- "same", always describing the solute concentration of the solution the cell sits in.

Picture
The technical version

Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane from a region of higher water concentration (lower solute) to lower water concentration (higher solute). Tonicity describes the solute concentration of a solution relative to the cell: hypertonic (higher solute outside, net water efflux), hypotonic (lower outside, net influx; lysis in animal cells, turgor in walled cells), isotonic (no net movement). Freeman also introduces water potential in plant contexts. Osmoregulation is the control of water balance.

Membrane proteins and transportchannel · carrier · pump · passive vs active transport

In plain words

Three kinds of door. A channel is a tunnel through the membrane shaped for one thing, often an ion; when it's open, that thing flows through down its gradient, no energy needed. A carrier grabs its passenger on one side, changes shape, and releases it on the other, also down the gradient, no energy needed; glucose gets in this way. Both of those are passive transport: the gradient does the work. A pump is a carrier that burns energy (ATP, the cell's fuel) to move something the wrong way, from low concentration to high, uphill. That is active transport, and it's how a cell keeps sodium out and potassium in even though both are constantly leaking back.

Picture
The technical version

Passive transport moves solutes down their electrochemical gradient without energy input: simple diffusion through the bilayer, or facilitated diffusion through channel proteins (aqueous pores, often gated) and carrier proteins (which bind the solute and undergo conformational change; e.g. GLUT glucose transporters). Active transport moves solutes against their gradient using energy: primary active transport hydrolyses ATP directly (the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP); secondary active transport couples uphill movement of one solute to downhill movement of another (cotransport). Bulk transport (endocytosis, exocytosis) moves material in vesicles and appears in Chapter 7.

Chapter 7Inside the Cell

The forest

A eukaryotic cell is a factory with departments, and this chapter is the floor plan. Each organelle is a membrane-wrapped room with one job, and the rooms are connected by a shipping system. The nucleus holds the master plans (DNA) and sends out working copies. Ribosomes read the copies and build proteins. Proteins meant for export or for the membrane go through the endoplasmic reticulum, get finished and sorted in the Golgi, and are shipped in little membrane bubbles (vesicles) to where they're needed. Mitochondria burn fuel for power. Lysosomes are recycling. The cytoskeleton is the scaffolding and the rail network everything moves along.

If you learn the map, every later chapter is a visit to one room. Chapter 9 is the mitochondrion. Chapter 10 is the chloroplast. Chapters 15 to 17 are the nucleus and the ribosome.

One sentence: a factory of membrane-walled rooms, with plans in the nucleus, assembly on ribosomes, and shipping through the ER, Golgi and vesicles.

Organellecytoplasm · cytosol · the floor plan

In plain words

An organelle ("little organ") is a structure inside a cell with a specific job, usually wrapped in its own membrane so its chemistry stays separate from everything else. The cytoplasm is everything inside the cell membrane except the nucleus; the cytosol is the watery fluid part of that, the stuff the organelles float in. Keeping jobs in separate rooms is the whole advantage of being a eukaryote: the recycling acid in a lysosome can't dissolve the cell, because it's walled off.

Picture
The technical version

Organelles are membrane-bound compartments with specialised functions (the term is sometimes extended to non-membranous structures like ribosomes). Cytoplasm is all contents inside the plasma membrane excluding the nucleus; cytosol is its aqueous phase. Compartmentalisation allows incompatible reactions to occur simultaneously, concentrates reactants, and enables regulation. Plant cells additionally have a cell wall, chloroplasts, and a large central vacuole.

Nucleusnuclear envelope · nuclear pores · chromosome · nucleolus

In plain words

The room where the master plans are kept. The DNA is in there, wound up with proteins into chromosomes, and the nucleus is wrapped in a double membrane (the nuclear envelope) with holes in it, the nuclear pores, which are gateways that check what goes in and out. Plans never leave; copies (RNA) go out through the pores, and proteins that work in the nucleus come in through them, carrying a molecular "address label" that the pore recognises. The nucleolus is a dense patch inside where ribosomes are put together.

Picture
The technical version

The nucleus is bounded by the nuclear envelope, two membranes continuous with the ER, perforated by nuclear pore complexes that mediate regulated bidirectional transport. Chromosomes are linear DNA molecules complexed with histone proteins (chromatin). The nucleolus is the site of ribosomal RNA synthesis and ribosome subunit assembly. Nuclear import requires a nuclear localization signal (NLS), a short amino acid sequence recognised by importin proteins; Freeman describes the classic experiment that identified it. The nuclear lamina provides structural support.

Ribosomefree vs bound

In plain words

The machine that builds proteins. It's a two-piece molecular assembly, made of RNA and protein, that clamps onto a working copy of a gene (messenger RNA) and reads it three letters at a time, adding one amino acid per three letters until the chain is done. Ribosomes floating free in the cytosol make proteins that will stay in the cytosol. Ribosomes that dock onto the endoplasmic reticulum make proteins headed for a membrane or for export. They are not wrapped in a membrane, and bacteria have them too, which is one reason many antibiotics work: they jam bacterial ribosomes but not ours.

Picture
The technical version

Ribosomes are ribonucleoprotein complexes of a large and a small subunit, each composed of rRNA and proteins, assembled in the nucleolus. They catalyse translation of mRNA into polypeptide (Chapter 17); the peptidyl transferase activity resides in rRNA (a ribozyme). Free ribosomes synthesise cytosolic, nuclear, mitochondrial and chloroplast proteins; ribosomes that bind the ER after the nascent chain displays an ER signal sequence synthesise secreted, membrane, and lysosomal proteins. Prokaryotic (70S) and eukaryotic (80S) ribosomes differ, a basis for antibiotic selectivity.

Endoplasmic reticulumrough ER · smooth ER · lumen

In plain words

A vast network of folded membrane sheets and tubes spreading out from the nucleus; the name means "network inside the cytoplasm." The part studded with ribosomes is the rough ER, and it's where proteins for export and for membranes are made and fed into the space inside the sheets (the lumen), where they fold and get their first sugars attached. The part without ribosomes is the smooth ER, which makes lipids, stores calcium, and, in liver cells, breaks down drugs and poisons. Think of the rough ER as the factory floor for shipped goods and the smooth ER as the chemical plant.

Picture
The technical version

The endoplasmic reticulum is a continuous membrane system of cisternae and tubules enclosing the ER lumen, continuous with the outer nuclear membrane. Rough ER bears bound ribosomes; nascent polypeptides with an ER signal sequence are threaded into the lumen (or membrane) co-translationally, where they fold with chaperone assistance and receive N-linked glycosylation. Smooth ER lacks ribosomes and is the site of phospholipid and steroid synthesis, Ca²⁺ sequestration (sarcoplasmic reticulum in muscle), and detoxification by cytochrome P450 enzymes in hepatocytes.

Golgi apparatus and vesiclescis and trans faces · secretion · exocytosis

In plain words

The finishing and sorting department. The Golgi is a stack of flattened membrane pancakes. Proteins arrive from the ER in little membrane bubbles called vesicles, which fuse into the near side of the stack (the cis face); as they pass through, the proteins get their sugars trimmed and finished and get sorted by destination; and at the far side (the trans face) they bud off in new vesicles addressed to the cell membrane, to a lysosome, or to be released outside. Releasing a vesicle's contents outside the cell, by fusing the vesicle with the cell membrane, is called exocytosis. A vesicle is just a tiny bubble of membrane; it's how the cell moves anything membrane-wrapped from room to room.

Picture
The technical version

The Golgi apparatus is a polarised stack of cisternae; the cis-Golgi network receives transport vesicles from the ER and the trans-Golgi network sorts cargo into vesicles for the plasma membrane, lysosomes, or secretory granules. Within the stack, glycoproteins are modified (glycan trimming and addition) and lysosomal enzymes are tagged with mannose-6-phosphate. Vesicle budding and fusion are mediated by coat proteins and SNAREs. Exocytosis fuses vesicles with the plasma membrane to release contents; endocytosis (phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor-mediated) internalises material into vesicles.

The endomembrane systemsecretory pathway · signal sequence · the pulse-chase experiment

In plain words

The nucleus, ER, Golgi, vesicles, lysosomes and the cell membrane are one connected shipping network, and the chapter's centrepiece is the route a protein takes through it. A protein meant for export starts on a ribosome with a short "ship me" tag at its front, the signal sequence. The tag gets the ribosome docked onto the ER and the protein threaded inside. From the ER it travels by vesicle to the Golgi, gets finished, and is shipped by vesicle to the membrane and out. Biologists worked this out by feeding cells radioactive amino acids for a moment (the pulse), then watching where the radioactivity showed up over the following minutes (the chase): first ER, then Golgi, then vesicles, then outside.

Picture
The technical version

The endomembrane system comprises the nuclear envelope, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vesicles and plasma membrane, connected by vesicular traffic. Secreted and membrane proteins carry an N-terminal ER signal sequence recognised by the signal recognition particle (SRP), which halts translation and docks the ribosome on the ER; the chain is threaded through a translocon into the lumen (signal hypothesis, Blobel). Palade's pulse-chase experiments with radiolabelled amino acids in pancreatic cells established the sequence rough ER → Golgi → secretory vesicles → exocytosis. Lysosomal proteins are diverted at the trans-Golgi by mannose-6-phosphate tags.

Lysosome and vacuoleautophagy · phagocytosis · turgor

In plain words

The lysosome is the recycling centre: a membrane bag full of acid and digestive enzymes that take apart worn-out organelles, swallowed bacteria, and anything else the cell wants to break down into reusable pieces. Keeping that acid in its own bag is why the cell doesn't digest itself. Plant cells have a different big bag, the vacuole, which is mostly water and dissolved stuff; when full it presses outward against the cell wall and keeps the plant stiff (turgor). A wilted plant is one whose vacuoles have lost water.

Picture
Hold this image: the lysosome is a stomach in a bag. The vacuole is a water balloon that keeps a plant standing up; let the water out and the plant droops.
The technical version

Lysosomes are acidic (pH ~5) organelles containing hydrolytic enzymes (acid hydrolases) that degrade macromolecules delivered by endocytosis, phagocytosis, or autophagy (the engulfment and recycling of the cell's own components). The proton pump in the lysosomal membrane maintains the low pH. Plant cell vacuoles store water, ions, pigments and defensive compounds and generate turgor pressure; they also perform lysosome-like digestion.

Mitochondrioncristae · matrix

In plain words

The power plant. Mitochondria take the fuel from food (mostly glucose, broken down first in the cytosol) and oxygen, and turn them into ATP, the energy currency every other process spends. Each one has two membranes: a smooth outer one, and an inner one folded into deep pleats called cristae, because the machinery that makes ATP sits in that inner membrane and folding it multiplies the surface. The space inside the inner membrane is the matrix. Mitochondria have their own small DNA and their own ribosomes, a leftover from their origin as free-living bacteria that were swallowed by an ancestor of ours and stayed. Chapter 9 is entirely about what happens inside them.

Picture
The technical version

Mitochondria are double-membrane organelles; the inner membrane is folded into cristae and houses the electron transport chain and ATP synthase, while the matrix contains the citric acid cycle enzymes, mitochondrial DNA (circular) and ribosomes. They are the site of aerobic respiration (Chapter 9), producing ATP by oxidative phosphorylation. Their circular genome, bacterial-type ribosomes, and division by fission support the endosymbiotic theory of origin from an α-proteobacterium. Numbers per cell scale with energy demand.

Chloroplastthylakoid · stroma · plastid

In plain words

The solar panel, found in plant and algal cells only. Chloroplasts capture light and use it to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water. Like mitochondria they have two outer membranes, their own DNA, and a bacterial ancestor. Inside, instead of pleats, they have stacks of flat green discs (thylakoids) where the light is captured, floating in a fluid (the stroma) where the sugar is assembled. The green is chlorophyll, the pigment that absorbs the light. Chapter 10 is entirely about what happens in here.

Picture
The technical version

Chloroplasts are plastids bounded by a double envelope, containing a third membrane system of thylakoids (often stacked as grana) suspended in the stroma. The light-dependent reactions of photosynthesis occur in thylakoid membranes (chlorophyll, photosystems, ATP synthase); the Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma. Chloroplasts contain circular DNA and ribosomes, consistent with endosymbiotic origin from a cyanobacterium. Other plastids store starch (amyloplasts) or pigments (chromoplasts).

Cytoskeletonactin filaments · intermediate filaments · microtubules · motor proteins · cilia and flagella

In plain words

A cell isn't a bag of soup; it has an internal frame of protein fibres that gives it shape, holds organelles in place, and serves as a rail system. Three kinds, by thickness. Actin filaments are the thinnest and sit just under the membrane; they let the cell change shape, crawl, and pinch in two when it divides. Intermediate filaments are the ropes that give mechanical strength. Microtubules are the thickest, hollow tubes that radiate out from a hub near the nucleus; they are the rails along which motor proteins (kinesin, dynein) carry vesicles and organelles, walking step by step and burning ATP. Microtubules also form the core of cilia and flagella, the whips a cell beats to move itself or move fluid past it.

Picture
The technical version

Actin filaments (microfilaments, ~7 nm): polymers of actin; cortex beneath the plasma membrane; cell shape, amoeboid movement, cytokinesis, muscle contraction with myosin. Intermediate filaments (~10 nm): diverse proteins (keratins, lamins); tensile strength. Microtubules (~25 nm): hollow tubes of α/β-tubulin dimers, nucleated at the microtubule-organising centre (centrosome); dynamic instability; tracks for kinesin (toward plus end) and dynein (toward minus end); form the mitotic spindle and the 9+2 axoneme of cilia and flagella. Motor proteins hydrolyse ATP to move.

Cell wall and extracellular matrixwhy cells are small (surface area to volume)

In plain words

Outside the membrane, plant cells, fungi and bacteria have a stiff wall (cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi, peptidoglycan in bacteria) that resists the pressure of the water inside and gives the cell its fixed shape. Animal cells have no wall; instead they sit in a mesh of protein fibres they secrete, the extracellular matrix, which holds tissues together. And a note on size: cells are tiny because everything they need comes in through the surface, but the demand comes from the whole volume. Double a cell's width and its surface goes up four times but its volume goes up eight; past a certain size the surface can't feed the inside. That is why big organisms are made of many small cells, not a few big ones.

Picture
The technical version

Cell walls: cellulose microfibrils in a matrix (plants, with a primary wall and often a lignified secondary wall); chitin (fungi); peptidoglycan (bacteria). The extracellular matrix of animal cells is a network of collagen and other fibrous proteins in a proteoglycan gel, linked to the cytoskeleton via integrins (Chapter 11). Surface-area-to-volume ratio scales as 1/r for a sphere, constraining cell size because exchange across the membrane must supply a metabolism that scales with volume; large cells compensate with elongation, folds, or internal membranes.

Chapter 9Cellular Respiration and Fermentation

The forest

This is the chapter about how a cell gets energy out of food, and it has a reputation for being the hardest in the course because of the number of molecule names. Ignore the names on the first pass. The story is simple: a cell takes glucose apart in stages, and at each stage it skims off a little energy. Most of that energy is carried, not as ATP directly, but as electrons riding on carrier molecules. At the end, those electrons are handed down a chain of proteins in the mitochondrion to oxygen, and the energy they give up on the way is used to pump protons across a membrane. The protons flowing back through a turbine-like enzyme make most of the ATP. That is why you breathe: oxygen is the final place the electrons go.

Four stages, four rooms: glycolysis in the cytosol (glucose is split in half), pyruvate processing and the citric acid cycle in the mitochondrial matrix (the halves are taken fully apart, carbon leaves as CO₂, electrons are loaded onto carriers), and the electron transport chain in the inner membrane (the carriers unload, oxygen accepts, ATP is made). If there is no oxygen, the chain stops, and fermentation is the emergency workaround that keeps the first stage running.

The course skips Chapter 8, but two ideas from it are load-bearing here: what ATP is, and what it means to move electrons. They are the first two cards.

One sentence: take glucose apart, load the electrons onto carriers, hand them down a chain to oxygen, and let the proton flow make ATP.

ATP · Photosynthesis and Cellular Respiration

ATPfrom the skipped Chapter 8 · energy currency · phosphorylation

In plain words

ATP is the cell's rechargeable battery. It is a nucleotide (adenine, ribose, like the ones in Chapter 4) with three phosphates in a row. Three negative phosphates crammed together repel each other, so the bond holding the last one on is under strain, and breaking it off releases energy the way a compressed spring does. The cell spends ATP by snapping off that last phosphate, leaving ADP (two phosphates), and very often sticks that phosphate onto another molecule to energise it; that is called phosphorylation. Respiration is the process of charging the battery back up: putting the third phosphate back on ADP. You make and use roughly your own body weight in ATP every day.

Picture
The technical version

Adenosine triphosphate: adenine, ribose, and three phosphate groups linked by phosphoanhydride bonds. Hydrolysis of the terminal phosphate (ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ) is strongly exergonic (ΔG ≈ −30 kJ/mol under standard conditions, more in cells) because it relieves electrostatic repulsion and the products are stabilised by resonance and hydration. Cells couple this exergonic reaction to endergonic ones, usually by transferring the phosphate to a substrate or protein (phosphorylation), which changes its shape or reactivity. ATP is regenerated from ADP by substrate-level phosphorylation and, mostly, by oxidative phosphorylation.

Redox and electron carriersfrom Chapter 8 · oxidation · reduction · NAD⁺/NADH · FADH₂

In plain words

Chemical energy is really about where electrons sit. When a molecule loses electrons it is oxidised; when it gains them it is reduced; the two always happen together (one molecule's loss is another's gain), so the pair is a redox reaction. The mnemonic is OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain. Glucose is a molecule whose electrons are held loosely and at high energy; oxygen holds electrons tightly. Respiration is moving electrons from glucose to oxygen in small, controlled steps and catching the energy on the way. The electrons don't travel alone: they are picked up by carrier molecules, mainly NAD⁺, which becomes NADH when it's loaded (and FAD, which becomes FADH₂). Think of NADH as a wheelbarrow full of energetic electrons, pushed from the early stages to the mitochondrion's inner membrane.

Picture
The technical version

Oxidation is loss of electrons (or, equivalently in biology, loss of hydrogen atoms or gain of oxygen); reduction is gain of electrons. Redox reactions are coupled. Electrons in C–H bonds are at higher potential energy than in C–O or O–H bonds, so oxidising glucose to CO₂ and H₂O is highly exergonic. NAD⁺ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) accepts two electrons and one proton to form NADH; FAD accepts two electrons and two protons to form FADH₂. These reduced coenzymes donate electrons to the electron transport chain. NADP⁺/NADPH is the analogous carrier in photosynthesis.

Cellular respiration, the mapfour stages · where each happens · what each makes

In plain words

The overall equation is glucose plus oxygen goes to carbon dioxide plus water, with energy captured as ATP. It happens in four stages, and knowing which room each one is in gets you halfway through the exam. Glycolysis, in the cytosol, splits one glucose into two pyruvate and makes a little ATP and NADH. Pyruvate processing, in the mitochondrial matrix, strips a carbon off each pyruvate as CO₂ and loads more NADH. The citric acid cycle, also in the matrix, takes the remaining carbons apart completely, releasing the rest of the CO₂ and loading most of the NADH. The electron transport chain, in the inner membrane, unloads all that NADH onto oxygen and makes most of the ATP. The CO₂ you breathe out comes from stages two and three; the oxygen you breathe in is used only in stage four.

Picture
The technical version

Aerobic respiration oxidises glucose completely: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O, ΔG ≈ −2870 kJ/mol. Stages: (1) glycolysis, cytosol, glucose → 2 pyruvate, net 2 ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation and 2 NADH; (2) pyruvate oxidation, matrix, 2 pyruvate → 2 acetyl-CoA + 2 CO₂ + 2 NADH; (3) citric acid (Krebs, TCA) cycle, matrix, 2 acetyl-CoA → 4 CO₂, 6 NADH, 2 FADH₂, 2 ATP (as GTP); (4) electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation, inner membrane, reoxidising 10 NADH and 2 FADH₂ with O₂ as terminal acceptor to yield roughly 26 ATP. Modern estimates give about 30 ATP per glucose rather than the older 36–38. Fats and proteins enter at intermediate points.

Glycolysispyruvate · substrate-level phosphorylation

In plain words

"Glyco-lysis" means sugar-splitting. Ten enzyme steps in the cytosol take one six-carbon glucose and cut it into two three-carbon pieces called pyruvate. The odd part is that it costs two ATP up front to get glucose primed, then pays back four, so the net is two ATP per glucose, plus two loaded NADH. It needs no oxygen and every living thing on Earth does it, which is the strongest hint that it is the oldest part of the whole process. The ATP here is made the direct way, by an enzyme physically transferring a phosphate from a sugar fragment onto ADP; that is called substrate-level phosphorylation, to distinguish it from the turbine method in stage four.

Picture
The technical version

Glycolysis: ten cytosolic reactions converting glucose to two pyruvate. Energy-investment phase: two ATP phosphorylate glucose (hexokinase) and fructose-6-phosphate (phosphofructokinase, the key regulated step, inhibited by ATP: feedback inhibition). Energy-payoff phase: glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate is oxidised (NAD⁺ → NADH) and two substrate-level phosphorylations per triose yield 4 ATP. Net: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate. Occurs in all domains; oxygen-independent.

Pyruvate processing and the citric acid cycleacetyl-CoA · Krebs cycle · where CO₂ comes from

In plain words

Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion. First a carbon is clipped off each one and leaves as CO₂, and the two-carbon piece that's left is attached to a helper molecule (coenzyme A) to make acetyl-CoA; an NADH is loaded in the process. Then the two-carbon piece is fed into the citric acid cycle: it's joined to a four-carbon molecule to make a six-carbon one (citrate), and over eight steps that gets taken apart, two carbons leaving as CO₂, until the four-carbon starting molecule is back and can go again. That's why it's a cycle. Each turn loads three NADH and one FADH₂ and makes one ATP. Since one glucose gives two acetyl-CoA, the cycle turns twice per glucose. At this point all six carbons of the glucose have left as CO₂, and nearly all the energy is sitting in loaded carriers.

Picture
The technical version

Pyruvate is transported into the matrix and oxidatively decarboxylated by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex: pyruvate + NAD⁺ + CoA → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH. In the citric acid cycle, acetyl-CoA condenses with oxaloacetate (4 C) to citrate (6 C); two decarboxylations and four oxidations regenerate oxaloacetate, yielding per turn 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 GTP (≈ ATP) and 2 CO₂. Regulated by feedback (NADH and ATP inhibit key dehydrogenases). Intermediates also feed biosynthesis, so the cycle is a metabolic hub.

Electron transport chaininner membrane · proton gradient · why oxygen

In plain words

The loaded carriers (NADH, FADH₂) arrive at the inner mitochondrial membrane, which is studded with a series of protein complexes. They hand their electrons to the first complex, and the electrons then get passed down the line, complex to complex, each one holding them a bit more tightly than the last, like water going down a staircase. At each step some energy is released, and the complexes use it to pump protons (H⁺) out of the matrix into the space between the two membranes. At the bottom of the staircase the electrons are handed to oxygen, which grabs two protons as well and becomes water. If there's no oxygen, the last step can't happen, the whole staircase backs up, and everything upstream stops. That is what suffocation is at the molecular level. Note what the chain does not do: it makes no ATP itself. It makes a proton gradient.

Picture
The technical version

The ETC comprises four inner-membrane complexes (I–IV) plus the mobile carriers ubiquinone (Q) and cytochrome c. NADH donates electrons to complex I, FADH₂ to complex II; electrons pass down a series of redox centres of increasing electronegativity (reduction potential) to complex IV, which reduces O₂ to H₂O. Complexes I, III and IV use the released free energy to pump H⁺ from the matrix to the intermembrane space, generating a proton-motive force (chemical plus electrical gradient). Cyanide blocks complex IV; without a terminal acceptor, NADH cannot be reoxidised and upstream stages halt. Some anaerobic prokaryotes use other terminal acceptors (nitrate, sulfate).

Chemiosmosis and ATP synthaseoxidative phosphorylation · the two ways to make ATP

In plain words

Now the protons pumped out by the chain are crowded on one side of the membrane and want back in, and there's only one way through: an enzyme called ATP synthase, which is literally a molecular turbine. Protons flowing through it make its shaft spin, and the spinning presses phosphate onto ADP to make ATP. Energy stored as a proton pile, cashed as ATP by a turbine: that process is chemiosmosis, and ATP made this way is called oxidative phosphorylation, because the electrons that pumped the protons came from oxidising food. It makes about nine-tenths of your ATP. The other tenth, from glycolysis and the citric acid cycle, is substrate-level phosphorylation, the direct hand-off. Exams love asking which is which.

Picture
The technical version

Chemiosmosis (Mitchell, 1961) is ATP synthesis driven by a transmembrane proton gradient. ATP synthase is a rotary motor: H⁺ flow through the membrane-embedded F₀ subunit rotates a central stalk that induces conformational changes in the F₁ catalytic subunits, which bind ADP + Pᵢ and release ATP. Oxidative phosphorylation = ETC + chemiosmosis, yielding about 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH₂. Substrate-level phosphorylation transfers a phosphate directly from a phosphorylated intermediate to ADP (glycolysis, citric acid cycle). Uncouplers (e.g. DNP) dissipate the gradient as heat; brown fat does this deliberately with UCP1.

Fermentationanaerobic · lactic acid · ethanol · why it exists

In plain words

Without oxygen the electron transport chain stalls, and the problem that causes isn't the lost ATP; it's that every NAD⁺ in the cell quickly ends up loaded as NADH with nowhere to unload. Glycolysis needs empty NAD⁺ to run, so it would stop too, and then there'd be no ATP at all. Fermentation is the fix: take the electrons off NADH and dump them onto pyruvate, freeing NAD⁺ so glycolysis can keep going. It makes no extra ATP, just the two from glycolysis, and it produces waste. In your muscles the waste is lactate (lactic acid fermentation, the burn in a sprint). In yeast it's ethanol and CO₂ (alcoholic fermentation: beer, wine, and the bubbles in bread). Fermentation is not a lesser respiration; it's glycolysis with a way of recycling its own carriers.

Picture
The technical version

Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ from NADH by transferring electrons to an organic acceptor derived from pyruvate, allowing glycolysis (and its 2 ATP) to continue without oxygen. Lactic acid fermentation: pyruvate + NADH → lactate + NAD⁺ (muscle under anaerobic load, lactic acid bacteria). Alcoholic fermentation: pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO₂, then acetaldehyde + NADH → ethanol + NAD⁺ (yeast). Fermentation yields ~2 ATP per glucose versus ~30 for aerobic respiration, so obligate fermenters must process far more glucose. Anaerobic respiration proper (ETC with a non-oxygen terminal acceptor) is distinct from fermentation.

Chapter 10Photosynthesis

The forest

Photosynthesis is respiration run backwards, with sunlight paying the bill. Plants take carbon dioxide and water and build sugar, and the energy to do it comes from light. The chapter has two halves that map onto two places in the chloroplast. In the thylakoid membranes, light is caught by chlorophyll, its energy is used to rip electrons out of water (which is where the oxygen you breathe comes from), and those electrons ride a chain, just like in the mitochondrion, to make ATP and to load a carrier called NADPH. In the stroma, the Calvin cycle spends that ATP and NADPH to grab CO₂ out of the air and stitch it into sugar.

So: light reactions make the energy (ATP, NADPH) and release oxygen; the Calvin cycle uses the energy to make sugar. Nearly every exam question is about which half does what, where it happens, and what goes in and out.

One sentence: light splits water and charges the batteries; the Calvin cycle spends them to turn CO₂ into sugar.

Photosynthesis and Cellular Respiration · Autotrophs and Heterotrophs

Photosynthesis, the maplight reactions · Calvin cycle · what goes where

In plain words

The overall equation is CO₂ plus water plus light gives sugar plus oxygen, the exact reverse of respiration. It runs as two linked processes inside the chloroplast. The light reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes (the green discs): light in, water split, oxygen out, and two energy carriers charged, ATP and NADPH. The Calvin cycle happens in the stroma (the fluid around the discs): it takes the ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, uses them to fix CO₂ into a three-carbon sugar, and hands the spent ADP and NADP⁺ back to be recharged. The two halves are a loop: the light reactions can't run without empty carriers coming back, and the Calvin cycle can't run without charged ones arriving. Neither half needs the sun directly except the first; the "dark reactions" name for the Calvin cycle is old and misleading, since it runs in daylight.

Picture
The technical version

Photosynthesis: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂. The light-dependent reactions in thylakoid membranes convert light energy to chemical energy as ATP and NADPH, oxidising water to O₂. The light-independent reactions (Calvin cycle) in the stroma reduce CO₂ to carbohydrate using that ATP and NADPH, regenerating ADP and NADP⁺. Photosynthesis is endergonic overall (ΔG ≈ +2870 kJ/mol glucose), the mirror of respiration. The O₂ released derives from water, not CO₂ (shown by isotope labelling).

Light and pigmentschlorophyll · photon · absorption spectrum · why plants are green

In plain words

Light comes in packets called photons, and shorter-wavelength light (blue) carries more energy per packet than longer (red). A pigment is a molecule that absorbs some wavelengths and not others; the ones it doesn't absorb bounce off, and that's the colour you see. Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red strongly and barely absorbs green, so green is reflected, so plants look green. When a chlorophyll absorbs a photon, one of its electrons is kicked up to a higher-energy state, and that excited electron is the whole starting point of photosynthesis: the light reactions are about catching it before it falls back. Other pigments (carotenoids, the orange ones you see in autumn once the chlorophyll is gone) absorb in the gap and pass the energy along.

Picture
The technical version

Photon energy is inversely proportional to wavelength (E = hc/λ). Chlorophyll a and b absorb maximally in the blue-violet (~430–450 nm) and red (~640–680 nm) regions; carotenoids absorb blue-green and also protect against photodamage. The absorption spectrum of chlorophyll and the action spectrum of photosynthesis (Engelmann's experiment) coincide. Absorption promotes an electron to an excited state; the energy can be lost as heat, re-emitted as fluorescence, transferred by resonance to a neighbouring pigment, or, in a reaction centre, used to drive electron transfer to an acceptor.

The light reactionsphotosystem II and I · splitting water · NADPH · photophosphorylation

In plain words

Chlorophyll molecules are grouped into two kinds of assembly in the thylakoid membrane, photosystem II and photosystem I (named in order of discovery, not order of use; II goes first). Light hits photosystem II and knocks an excited electron loose. To replace it, the photosystem rips an electron out of water, and water torn apart that way falls into oxygen and protons; that oxygen leaves the plant. The excited electron rides a short transport chain, pumping protons into the thylakoid interior as it goes, exactly like the mitochondrial chain, and those protons flow back out through an ATP synthase to make ATP. The electron, now lower in energy, arrives at photosystem I, gets a second kick from a second photon, and is finally handed to NADP⁺ to make NADPH. Two light hits, one electron, one water molecule: out come oxygen, ATP and NADPH.

Picture
The technical version

Non-cyclic electron flow (the Z scheme): photosystem II (P680) absorbs light and passes an excited electron to plastoquinone; the oxygen-evolving complex splits water (2 H₂O → O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻) to replace it. Electrons pass through the cytochrome b₆f complex to plastocyanin, pumping H⁺ into the thylakoid lumen; the resulting gradient drives ATP synthase (photophosphorylation). Photosystem I (P700) re-excites the electron and, via ferredoxin, NADP⁺ reductase forms NADPH. Cyclic electron flow around PS I makes extra ATP without NADPH to balance the Calvin cycle's demand. Antenna complexes funnel energy to reaction centres by resonance transfer.

The Calvin cyclecarbon fixation · rubisco · RuBP · G3P

In plain words

This is the sugar factory in the stroma, and it runs in three phases. Fixation: an enzyme called rubisco grabs a CO₂ from the air and bolts it onto a five-carbon molecule (RuBP), making an unstable six-carbon thing that immediately breaks into two three-carbon pieces. Reduction: those pieces are energised with ATP and loaded with electrons from NADPH, which turns them into a three-carbon sugar, G3P; that is the product, and the plant builds glucose and everything else from it. Regeneration: most of the G3P is spent rebuilding RuBP so the cycle can grab the next CO₂. For every three CO₂ fixed, one G3P leaves as profit and the rest is reinvested. Rubisco is the most abundant protein on Earth and also famously slow and sloppy, which is the next card.

Picture
The technical version

Carbon fixation: rubisco (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) adds CO₂ to RuBP (5 C), producing an unstable 6 C intermediate that splits into two 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). Reduction: 3-PGA is phosphorylated by ATP and reduced by NADPH to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P). Regeneration: five of every six G3P are rearranged, using ATP, to regenerate three RuBP. Stoichiometry per G3P exported: 3 CO₂, 9 ATP, 6 NADPH. Two G3P make one glucose. G3P is also the branch point to sucrose (export) and starch (storage).

Photorespiration, C4 and CAMrubisco's mistake · hot, dry places

In plain words

Rubisco has a flaw: it can't tell CO₂ from O₂ very well, and when it grabs oxygen by mistake the plant loses carbon and energy cleaning up the result. That wasteful side-reaction is photorespiration, and it gets worse when it's hot and dry, because the plant closes the pores in its leaves to save water, CO₂ inside runs low, and oxygen from the light reactions builds up. Two groups of plants evolved workarounds. C4 plants (corn, sugar cane) grab CO₂ with a different, fussier enzyme in the outer leaf cells and pump it, concentrated, into inner cells where rubisco works. CAM plants (cacti, pineapple) open their pores only at night, store the CO₂ as an acid, and release it to rubisco by day with the pores shut. Both are ways of hand-feeding rubisco enough CO₂ that it stops making mistakes.

Picture
Hold this image: rubisco is a sorting worker who sometimes grabs the wrong box. C4 plants put a sharper-eyed worker at the door to pass it only the right boxes. CAM plants take deliveries at night, when the wrong boxes aren't around, and stock the shelf for the day shift.
The technical version

Rubisco's oxygenase activity fixes O₂ instead of CO₂, producing 2-phosphoglycolate; salvaging it (photorespiration) consumes ATP and releases CO₂, reducing net photosynthesis, particularly at high temperature and low intercellular CO₂ when stomata close. C4 plants spatially separate initial fixation (PEP carboxylase in mesophyll cells, forming a 4 C acid) from the Calvin cycle (bundle-sheath cells, where the acid is decarboxylated to concentrate CO₂ around rubisco). CAM plants separate the steps in time: stomata open at night, CO₂ stored as malate in vacuoles, released to the Calvin cycle by day. Both cost extra ATP but pay off in hot, dry conditions.

How the two chapters fit togetherrespiration and photosynthesis as mirror images

In plain words

Put the two equations side by side and they are the same line read in opposite directions. Photosynthesis takes CO₂ and water, adds light energy, and makes sugar and oxygen. Respiration takes sugar and oxygen and gives back CO₂ and water, releasing the energy as ATP. The electron story matches too: photosynthesis uses light to push electrons from water uphill into sugar; respiration lets them roll back downhill from sugar to oxygen, catching the energy on the way. Plants do both: they photosynthesise in daylight and respire all the time, like everything else alive. The oxygen you breathe is the leftover from water being split in a leaf, and the CO₂ you exhale is the leftover from sugar being taken apart in your mitochondria, which a leaf will grab next.

Picture
The technical version

Photosynthesis (6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂) is endergonic, storing light energy as chemical potential energy in reduced carbon; aerobic respiration (C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + ~30 ATP) is exergonic, releasing it. Both use membrane-bound electron transport chains and chemiosmotic ATP synthesis; both use nicotinamide carriers (NADPH in photosynthesis, NADH in respiration). Photoautotrophs perform both; heterotrophs only respiration. Together they drive the global carbon and oxygen cycles.

Chapter 11Cell–Cell Interactions

The forest

Up to now the cell has been alone. This chapter is about what happens when cells live next to each other, which every cell in your body does. Two questions. How do cells hold on to each other? With a mesh of protein outside (the extracellular matrix) and with three kinds of rivet between neighbours: ones that seal, ones that anchor, and ones that let small molecules pass straight from cell to cell. How do cells talk? One cell releases a chemical, the signal. It floats to another cell, which has a receptor shaped to catch exactly that signal. Catching it flips the receptor, and the flip sets off a chain of events inside the cell, the signal transduction pathway, which ends in a response: a gene switched on, an enzyme activated, the cell dividing or moving or dying.

The whole second half of the chapter is variations on that one sentence. The signal is outside; the response is inside; the receptor is the door; transduction is everything in between, and it usually amplifies the message, so one molecule outside can change a million inside.

One sentence: cells stick to each other with rivets, and talk to each other with a signal, a receptor, a relay, and a response.

Holding onextracellular matrix · tight junctions · desmosomes · gap junctions · plasmodesmata

In plain words

Cells in a tissue are held by two things. Outside them is the extracellular matrix, a felt of protein fibres (mostly collagen) that the cells themselves secrete and cling to. Between neighbours are three kinds of connection, each doing one job. Tight junctions stitch two cells together so nothing leaks between them; they make the lining of your gut watertight. Desmosomes are rivets: strong spot-welds that keep cells from tearing apart under strain, which is why skin holds together. Gap junctions are tiny tunnels straight from one cell's interior to the next, so small molecules and signals pass directly; heart muscle cells beat together because of them. Plant cells, which have walls, use plasmodesmata: channels through the wall that do the same job as gap junctions.

Picture
The technical version

The extracellular matrix (ECM) is a fibre-composite of collagen and other proteins in a proteoglycan gel, linked to the cytoskeleton via integrins (transmembrane receptors), which also transmit signals about attachment. Plant cell walls play the analogous role. Cell–cell junctions in animals: tight junctions (claudins, occludins) form sealing belts in epithelia; desmosomes (cadherins anchored to intermediate filaments) provide mechanical strength; gap junctions (connexons) are channels allowing ions and small molecules to pass, coordinating cells. Plasmodesmata are cytoplasmic channels through plant cell walls. Selective adhesion via cadherins lets cells sort into tissues.

Cell signalling, the mapsignal (ligand) · receptor · transduction · response

In plain words

Every signalling story has the same four beats. One cell releases a signal molecule (in the jargon, a ligand: anything that binds). It travels, sometimes across the body in the blood (a hormone), sometimes only to the cell next door. A receptor on the target cell catches it; receptor and signal fit like a lock and key, so only cells with that receptor "hear" that signal. Binding changes the receptor's shape, which starts a relay inside the cell, the signal transduction pathway. The relay ends in a response: something the cell does differently. The same signal can produce different responses in different cells, because what happens depends on what relay and what machinery that cell has. Adrenaline makes the heart beat faster and the liver release sugar: same signal, two receivers, two responses.

Picture
The technical version

Intercellular signals are classified by range: autocrine, paracrine (local), endocrine (hormones via the bloodstream), and direct contact. A ligand binds its receptor with high specificity and affinity, inducing a conformational change. Signal transduction converts the extracellular signal into an intracellular one, typically through a cascade that amplifies it and integrates it with other inputs. Responses include changes in enzyme activity, gene expression, cytoskeleton, or membrane transport. Specificity of response arises from the receptor and pathway components present in each cell type. Signalling is terminated by ligand removal, receptor inactivation, and enzymes that reverse each step.

Two kinds of receptorsurface receptors for polar signals · intracellular receptors for lipid-soluble signals

In plain words

Where the receptor sits depends on whether the signal can get through the membrane. Most signals are polar or big (proteins, peptides, adrenaline), so they can't cross the oily bilayer; their receptors are proteins sticking out of the cell surface, and the message has to be relayed inward. A few signals are lipids (steroid hormones like testosterone and estrogen) and slip straight through the membrane; their receptors are inside the cell, often in the nucleus, and the receptor–signal pair usually goes and switches genes on directly. Surface receptors give fast, short responses; intracellular ones give slower, longer ones, because changing gene expression takes time.

Picture
The technical version

Hydrophilic ligands (peptides, amines, growth factors) bind cell-surface receptors: G-protein-coupled receptors (which activate G proteins and effector enzymes), receptor tyrosine kinases (which dimerise and autophosphorylate), and ligand-gated ion channels. Lipid-soluble ligands (steroids, thyroid hormone, nitric oxide) cross the membrane and bind intracellular receptors; steroid receptors are ligand-activated transcription factors that bind DNA at hormone-response elements. Surface-receptor pathways act in seconds to minutes; nuclear-receptor pathways in hours.

Signal transductionsecond messengers · kinase cascade · phosphorylation switch · amplification

In plain words

Once the receptor is flipped, the message is passed along inside by a relay. Two common ways. One is a small molecule made in a burst, a second messenger, that spreads through the cell; the first messenger was the signal outside, this is the second one inside. The classic is cyclic AMP; another is calcium ions released from storage. The other way is a chain of enzymes called kinases, each of which switches the next one on by sticking a phosphate on it (phosphorylation; the reverse, taking it off, switches it back). Both ways amplify: one receptor activates many second messengers or many kinases, each of which activates many more. So a handful of hormone molecules outside can change the state of the whole cell. The chapter's examples all reduce to: receptor flips, relay runs, something downstream gets a phosphate or sees a burst of second messenger.

Picture
The technical version

Second messengers are small, rapidly diffusing intracellular molecules produced in response to receptor activation: cyclic AMP (made by adenylyl cyclase, activates protein kinase A), Ca²⁺ (released from the ER, binds calmodulin), IP₃ and diacylglycerol (from phospholipase C). Phosphorylation cascades: protein kinases add phosphates (from ATP) to serine, threonine or tyrosine, activating or inhibiting the next component; phosphatases reverse it. G proteins act as on/off switches using GTP/GDP. Each stage multiplies the number of activated molecules (amplification). Pathways cross-talk, and their termination is as regulated as their activation.

Chapter 12The Cell Cycle

The forest

Cells come from cells (Chapter 1), and this chapter is how. A cell copies its DNA, then divides so each half gets a full copy. The cycle has a long working-and-preparing phase (interphase, where the DNA is copied) and a short dividing phase (mitosis and cytokinesis, where the copies are pulled apart and the cell pinches in two). Mitosis is the choreography for getting the right chromosomes to the right side, and it comes with stage names the exam will ask about: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase.

The chapter's other theme is control. A cell must not divide unless its DNA is copied correctly and conditions are right, so there are checkpoints, and proteins that push or hold the cycle. When the brakes fail and a cell divides without permission, that is cancer, which is why this chapter matters beyond the exam.

One sentence: copy the chromosomes, line them up, pull the copies apart, pinch in two; checkpoints decide whether to go.

Mitosis · Chromosome Numbers

Chromosomechromatin · sister chromatids · centromere · DNA replication

In plain words

A chromosome is one very long DNA molecule wound around proteins, like thread on spools, so that two metres of DNA fits in a cell. Most of the time it's a loose tangle called chromatin, which is how it's read. Before the cell divides it copies each chromosome, and the copy stays attached to the original at a waist called the centromere; the two attached copies are sister chromatids, and together they make the X shape you've seen in pictures. Then, for division, the whole thing coils up tight enough to see under a microscope and to move without tangling. Humans have 46 chromosomes; after copying there are still 46, each now made of two chromatids.

Picture
The technical version

A eukaryotic chromosome is a single linear DNA molecule complexed with histones and other proteins (chromatin). During S phase, DNA replication produces two identical sister chromatids held together by cohesin at the centromere. At the centromere assembles the kinetochore, the attachment site for spindle microtubules. Chromatin condenses into compact chromosomes during prophase (condensin). Chromosome number is unchanged by replication; chromatid number doubles. Humans: 2n = 46.

The cell cycleinterphase (G₁, S, G₂) · M phase · checkpoints

In plain words

A dividing cell goes round a loop. Most of the loop is interphase, which is not a resting phase but the working one: the cell grows and does its job (G₁), copies all its DNA (S, for synthesis), then grows a bit more and checks the copies (G₂). Then comes the short M phase: mitosis, in which the copied chromosomes are separated, followed by cytokinesis, in which the cell itself splits. Cells that have stopped dividing, like most neurons, sit off the loop in a state called G₀. At three points in the loop there are checkpoints where the cell asks "is everything ready?" and won't proceed until it is: is the cell big enough and are conditions right (end of G₁), is the DNA copied and undamaged (end of G₂), are all chromosomes attached to the machinery (mid-mitosis)?

Picture
The technical version

Interphase: G₁ (growth, normal function), S (DNA synthesis), G₂ (growth, preparation). M phase: mitosis (nuclear division) and cytokinesis (cytoplasmic division). G₀: non-dividing state. Checkpoints: G₁/S (cell size, nutrients, growth factors, DNA integrity; the "restriction point" past which the cell commits), G₂/M (replication complete, DNA undamaged), and the spindle assembly checkpoint at metaphase (all kinetochores attached under tension). Progression is driven by cyclin-dependent kinases (Cdks) activated by cyclins whose levels rise and fall through the cycle (MPF = M-phase cyclin–Cdk).

Mitosisprophase · metaphase · anaphase · telophase · cytokinesis · spindle

In plain words

Mitosis is the sorting step. Prophase: the copied chromosomes coil up into visible X shapes, the nuclear envelope breaks down, and a scaffold of microtubules called the spindle grows from two poles. Metaphase: the spindle fibres attach to each chromosome's waist and tug until every one is lined up along the middle. Anaphase: the waist connection is cut and the sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles, one of each pair to each side. Telophase: two new nuclear envelopes form around the two sets, and the chromosomes relax back into chromatin. Cytokinesis, overlapping the end: the cell pinches in two (animals) or builds a wall down the middle (plants). Result: two cells, each with the complete set the parent had. A mnemonic for the order: Please Make A Toast.

Picture
The technical version

Prophase: chromatin condenses; centrosomes separate and nucleate the mitotic spindle; nucleolus disappears. Prometaphase (often folded into prophase): nuclear envelope fragments; kinetochore microtubules attach. Metaphase: chromosomes align at the metaphase plate; spindle checkpoint monitors attachment. Anaphase: cohesin is cleaved (separase), sister chromatids separate and move poleward (anaphase A) while poles move apart (anaphase B). Telophase: nuclear envelopes re-form; chromosomes decondense. Cytokinesis: contractile ring of actin and myosin in animals (cleavage furrow); cell plate from Golgi vesicles in plants.

Control and cancercyclins and Cdks · tumor suppressors (p53) · oncogenes · why cancer is a cell-cycle disease

In plain words

The cycle is driven by pairs of proteins: a cyclin, whose amount rises and falls, and a kinase it switches on (a Cdk). When enough of the right cyclin builds up, its Cdk activates and pushes the cell through the next gate. Other proteins are brakes: p53, the most famous, halts the cycle when DNA is damaged and orders repair or, if the damage is too much, the cell's own death. Cancer is what happens when this control fails. A mutation that jams an accelerator on (an oncogene) or breaks a brake (a tumor suppressor gene like p53) lets the cell divide when it shouldn't, and each division can add more mutations. Most tumours have several such failures stacked up, which is why cancer is mostly a disease of age. Nearly every cancer drug works by hitting some part of this chapter.

Picture
The technical version

Cyclin-dependent kinases are activated by binding cyclins whose synthesis and degradation oscillate; G₁ cyclins drive passage through the restriction point, and M-phase cyclin–Cdk (MPF) triggers mitosis. Growth factors act through receptor tyrosine kinase and Ras pathways to promote G₁ cyclin expression. Tumor suppressors (p53, Rb) restrain progression; p53 induces p21 (a Cdk inhibitor), DNA repair, or apoptosis. Proto-oncogenes (Ras, Myc) become oncogenes by gain-of-function mutation. Cancer requires multiple hits: loss of growth control, evasion of apoptosis, telomerase reactivation, and, for malignancy, invasion and metastasis. Social controls (contact inhibition, anchorage dependence) are also lost.

Chapter 13Meiosis

The forest

Mitosis makes copies. Meiosis makes gametes: eggs and sperm, with half the chromosomes, so that when two meet the count comes back to normal. That is the whole reason it exists, and the structure follows from it. Your 46 chromosomes are really 23 pairs, one of each pair from each parent; the two members of a pair are homologous, meaning they carry the same genes in the same order, though possibly different versions. Meiosis separates the pairs (meiosis I), then separates the copies (meiosis II), so from one cell with 46 you get four cells with 23.

Along the way two things shuffle the deck: the pairs swap segments before they separate (crossing over), and which member of each pair goes to which side is random (independent assortment). That is why siblings differ, and it is why sex exists at all: it makes variation, and variation is what natural selection needs.

One sentence: halve the chromosome number in two rounds, shuffling as you go, so that fertilisation restores the count with a new combination.

Meiosis · Chromosome Numbers · Asexual and Sexual Reproduction

Diploid and haploidhomologous chromosomes · gametes · fertilisation · zygote

In plain words

Diploid means two of each chromosome, one from each parent; nearly every cell in you is diploid, with 46 (written 2n = 46). Haploid means one of each; eggs and sperm are haploid, with 23 (n = 23), and they are called gametes. The two chromosomes of a pair are homologous: same length, same genes at the same spots, but each may carry a different version of a gene, because one came from your mother and one from your father. Fertilisation fuses two haploid gametes into one diploid cell, the zygote, which then divides by mitosis a few trillion times to make a person. Meiosis is the step that makes gametes haploid, and if it didn't, the chromosome count would double every generation.

Picture
The technical version

Ploidy is the number of complete chromosome sets: diploid (2n) somatic cells, haploid (n) gametes. Homologous chromosomes (homologs) carry the same loci in the same order but may bear different alleles; they are not identical, unlike sister chromatids. Karyotype: the chromosome complement, in humans 22 autosome pairs plus sex chromosomes (XX or XY). Fertilisation restores diploidy and combines maternal and paternal genomes. Life cycles vary in where meiosis occurs (diploid-dominant in animals; alternation of generations in plants; haploid-dominant in many fungi).

Meiosis I and IIpairs separate, then copies separate · four cells from one

In plain words

Meiosis is two divisions in a row with only one round of DNA copying. Before it starts, every chromosome is copied, so each is an X of two sister chromatids, just as in mitosis. Meiosis I is the special one: homologous pairs find each other and line up side by side, and then the pairs are pulled apart, one whole X of each pair to each side. That halves the chromosome number: each daughter cell now has 23 X's, not 46. Meiosis II is just mitosis on those cells: the X's line up, the sister chromatids are separated, and each of the two cells splits into two. Four cells, each with 23 single chromosomes. The thing to hold onto: meiosis I separates homologs, meiosis II separates sisters. Mitosis only ever does the second.

Picture
The technical version

Meiosis I (reductional division): in prophase I homologs pair (synapsis) forming tetrads/bivalents joined by chiasmata; at metaphase I tetrads align; at anaphase I homologs separate (sister chromatids stay joined). Products are haploid but each chromosome is still two chromatids. Meiosis II (equational division) resembles mitosis: sisters separate. Outcome: four genetically distinct haploid cells. In human males all four become sperm; in females unequal cytokinesis yields one egg and polar bodies. Compare mitosis: one division, two identical diploid cells, no pairing, no crossing over.

Why siblings differcrossing over · independent assortment · random fertilisation

In plain words

Three shuffles. First, when the homologous pairs line up in meiosis I, they swap matching segments: a piece of the chromosome from your mother trades places with the same piece from your father. That is crossing over, and it means each chromosome that goes into a gamete is a patchwork of both grandparents. Second, when the pairs are pulled apart, which member goes left and which goes right is decided independently for each of the 23 pairs, like 23 coin flips; that is independent assortment, and it alone gives over eight million possible gametes. Third, which sperm meets which egg is random. Multiply those together and no two siblings (other than identical twins) have ever had the same genome. This is the point of sex: not more offspring, but different ones.

Picture
The technical version

Crossing over (recombination) occurs in prophase I between non-sister chromatids of homologs at chiasmata, producing recombinant chromosomes; the synaptonemal complex mediates pairing. Independent assortment: the orientation of each tetrad at metaphase I is random and independent of other tetrads, giving 2ⁿ possible gamete combinations (2²³ in humans, before recombination). Random fertilisation multiplies the combinations (≈ 7 × 10¹³ for one couple, ignoring crossing over). These sources of variation underlie Mendel's laws (Chapter 14) and provide the raw material for natural selection; hypotheses for the evolution of sex include the purifying-selection and changing-environment (Red Queen) explanations.

When meiosis goes wrongnondisjunction · trisomy · Down syndrome · aneuploidy

In plain words

Sometimes a pair fails to separate in meiosis I, or sisters fail to separate in meiosis II. That is nondisjunction. One gamete then has an extra chromosome and another is missing one. If a gamete with an extra copy is fertilised, the embryo has three of that chromosome instead of two: a trisomy. Most trisomies are not survivable; trisomy 21 is, and is Down syndrome. Extra or missing sex chromosomes (XXY, XO) are also survivable. The risk of nondisjunction rises with the age of the egg, which is why the incidence rises with maternal age; human eggs are arrested part-way through meiosis I from before birth until ovulation, sometimes for forty years.

Picture
Hold this image: a pair of shoes that should be split between two boxes, but both end up in one. One box now has three shoes; the other has one. The three-shoe box is a trisomy.
The technical version

Nondisjunction is failure of homologs (meiosis I) or sister chromatids (meiosis II) to separate, producing aneuploid gametes (n + 1, n − 1). Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome) is the commonest viable autosomal aneuploidy; trisomies 13 and 18 are usually lethal in infancy; sex-chromosome aneuploidies (XXY Klinefelter, XO Turner, XYY, XXX) are viable because of X inactivation and the small gene content of Y. Maternal age effect is attributed to deterioration of cohesin in oocytes arrested in prophase I. Polyploidy (whole extra sets) is lethal in humans but common in plants.

Chapter 14Mendel and the Gene

The forest

Mendel, a monk with a garden of pea plants in the 1860s, worked out the rules of inheritance before anyone knew genes or chromosomes existed. His results only make sense in the light of Chapter 13, and the chapter is really the two laid side by side. Each trait is controlled by a gene; you carry two copies of every gene, one from each parent; the two copies can be different versions (alleles); and when you make gametes the two copies separate, so each gamete gets one. That last sentence is meiosis I in Mendel's language. Some versions mask others when paired (dominant over recessive), which is why a trait can skip a generation and come back.

The tool for predicting the outcome of a cross is the Punnett square, a small grid that does the probability for you. Then the chapter explains the cases Mendel didn't see: versions that blend or both show, genes with many versions, traits controlled by many genes, and genes on the sex chromosomes.

One sentence: two copies of every gene, separated into gametes and recombined at random; dominant versions hide recessive ones.

Alleles and Genes · Monohybrid Crosses (Mendelian Inheritance) · Dihybrid Crosses (Mendelian Inheritance) · Multiple Alleles (ABO Blood Types) & Punnett Squares · Punnett Squares and Sex-Linked Traits · Pedigrees

The vocabulary of a crossgene · allele · genotype · phenotype · dominant · recessive · homozygous · heterozygous

In plain words

A gene is a stretch of DNA for one trait, at a fixed spot on a chromosome. An allele is one version of that gene; pea colour has a yellow allele and a green allele. Because you have two of each chromosome, you have two alleles of each gene. Your genotype is which two you have, written with letters: YY, Yy, or yy. Your phenotype is what you actually look like: yellow or green. If the two alleles are the same you are homozygous (YY or yy); if different, heterozygous (Yy). A dominant allele (capital letter) shows in the phenotype even when paired with a different one; a recessive allele (lowercase) shows only when there are two of it. So Yy looks yellow, and the green allele is hiding, ready to reappear in the next generation.

Picture
The technical version

A gene occupies a locus on a chromosome; alleles are alternative sequences at that locus. Diploid genotype: homozygous (two identical alleles) or heterozygous. Phenotype is the observable trait, a product of genotype and environment. Dominance describes the phenotype of the heterozygote: complete dominance when it matches one homozygote. Dominance is a property of the phenotype relationship, not of allele "strength" or frequency; recessive alleles are often loss-of-function. Wild-type denotes the common allele.

The Punnett squaremonohybrid cross · 3:1 · law of segregation · test cross

In plain words

A Punnett square is a grid that lists what each parent can put into a gamete along its two edges, then fills in every combination. Mendel's classic: cross two heterozygous yellow plants, Yy × Yy. Each parent makes gametes carrying Y or y in equal numbers (that's the law of segregation: the two alleles separate, one per gamete, which is meiosis I). The grid gives YY, Yy, yY, yy: three yellow to one green. That 3:1 ratio, from two yellow parents, is the signature of a hidden recessive. The grid is just probability made visible: each box is one quarter. A test cross uses it in reverse: cross a yellow plant of unknown genotype with a green one (yy); if any green offspring appear, the unknown parent was Yy.

Picture
The technical version

Mendel's principle of segregation: the two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation so each gamete carries one, a consequence of homolog separation at anaphase I. A monohybrid cross between heterozygotes gives a 1:2:1 genotypic and 3:1 phenotypic ratio; the F₁ of pure lines are all heterozygous. A test cross (unknown × homozygous recessive) reveals the unknown genotype. Probability rules: product rule for independent events (both gametes carry y: ½ × ½ = ¼), sum rule for mutually exclusive outcomes. Mendel's pure-breeding lines and large sample sizes were what made the ratios visible.

Two genes at oncedihybrid cross · 9:3:3:1 · law of independent assortment · linkage

In plain words

Mendel then followed two traits together, seed colour and seed shape, and found that they were inherited independently: whether a seed was yellow said nothing about whether it was round. That is the law of independent assortment, and it is Chapter 13's independent assortment in Mendel's language: the two genes sit on different chromosomes, and which allele of one goes into a gamete has nothing to do with which allele of the other. A dihybrid cross (YyRr × YyRr) needs a four-by-four grid and gives the famous 9:3:3:1 ratio: nine showing both dominant traits, three and three showing one each, one showing both recessive. The exception, which Mendel was lucky to miss, is when two genes sit close together on the same chromosome: they tend to travel together (they are linked), and only crossing over separates them.

Picture
The technical version

Principle of independent assortment: alleles of different genes assort independently during gamete formation, true for genes on different chromosomes (or far apart on one). A dihybrid heterozygote produces four gamete types in equal frequency; the F₂ phenotypic ratio is 9:3:3:1, derivable by the product rule from two 3:1 ratios. Linked genes (close loci on one chromosome) do not assort independently; parental combinations predominate and recombinant frequency measures map distance (Morgan, Sturtevant). Chromosome theory of inheritance (Sutton, Boveri): Mendel's factors are on chromosomes.

Beyond Mendelincomplete dominance · codominance · multiple alleles (blood types) · polygenic · sex-linked · pedigrees

In plain words

Mendel's peas were tidy; most traits aren't, and the chapter ends with the ways they aren't. Incomplete dominance: the heterozygote is in between (red × white snapdragons give pink). Codominance: both alleles show fully (the AB blood type has both A and B markers). Multiple alleles: a gene can have more than two versions in the population, though you still carry only two (blood type has three: A, B, and O, with O recessive to both). Polygenic: many genes each add a little, giving a smooth range rather than categories (height, skin colour). Sex-linked: genes on the X chromosome show differently in males, who have only one X and so have no second copy to mask a recessive allele; that is why colour blindness and haemophilia are mostly seen in males. A pedigree is a family tree with the trait marked in, used to work out which of these patterns is in play.

Picture
The technical version

Incomplete dominance: intermediate heterozygote phenotype (1:2:1 phenotypic ratio). Codominance: both alleles expressed (ABO: Iᴬ and Iᴮ codominant, both dominant to i). Multiple alleles exist at the population level. Pleiotropy: one gene, many traits. Epistasis: one gene masks another. Polygenic (quantitative) traits: many loci plus environment produce continuous variation. Sex linkage: X-linked recessive traits (haemophilia, red-green colour blindness) appear mainly in hemizygous males and are transmitted by carrier females; Y-linked traits pass father to son. Pedigree analysis distinguishes autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive and X-linked patterns.
